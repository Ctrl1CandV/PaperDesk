# Concept Drift: When Old Patterns Fail and How Systems Adapt

A system can receive data continuously while relying on an increasingly unsuitable prediction rule. Concept drift research asks how to detect changes in the data-generating process, update a model, and determine whether the update helps. It offers a foundation for thinking about long-running agents: receiving input, retaining history, and keeping judgments effective are distinct problems.

This guide covers **A Survey on Concept Drift Adaptation**, by João Gama, Indrė Žliobaitė, Albert Bifet, Mykola Pechenizkiy, and Abdelhamid Bouchachia. The formal publication appeared in **ACM Computing Surveys, 2014, 46(4), Article 44**. The source used for the Chinese guide was the authors' publicly available **44-page accepted manuscript, including its online appendix**, rather than the published typeset version. Its 2013 footer and placeholder DOI do not override the formal publication details. Page references below are physical PDF pages, counted from 1. [Publication record](https://doi.org/10.1145/2523813); [accepted manuscript, physical pages 1–2](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

This is a mainline guide to a classic survey, connecting its problems, methods, and evaluation. It offers neither a ranking of all current methods nor a direct solution to incorporating new observations during language-model generation. The teaching example and connections to agents are the guide's explanations, not conclusions validated by the survey.

## Has an observation changed, or has the rule producing observations changed?

In supervised prediction, $X$ denotes observable features and $y$ the label to be predicted. A system first sees the input and predicts; the true label may arrive later. Beyond a sequence of different values, the generating process can be described by a joint distribution $p_t(X,y)$ at time $t$. A change in that distribution over time is the survey's broad characterization of concept drift. [Accepted manuscript §2.1, physical pages 3–5](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

A teaching example illustrates the distinction. Someone walks from a doorway to a table: the current state and observations change, but the statistical relationship between position, action, and subsequent behavior need not change. By contrast, the same sensor readings might correspond to different failure risks before and after equipment wear. Studying drift requires specifying the distribution, time scale, and prediction target; a new scene alone is insufficient evidence.

The authors distinguish two cases. **Real concept drift** changes the conditional distribution $p_t(y\mid X)$: the same input can have different label probabilities, even if the input distribution $p_t(X)$ remains unchanged. Under the survey's main definition, **virtual drift** changes the input distribution while leaving the conditional relationship intact. Figure 1 illustrates this with two classes of points and a decision boundary: the middle panel changes the classification relationship, while the right panel changes the distribution of points but keeps the boundary. The authors also note that earlier literature uses “virtual drift” differently, so its definition needs checking in context. [Accepted manuscript §2.1 and Figure 1, physical pages 4–5](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Two distinctions matter. Monitoring inputs alone can miss real drift, so unchanged-looking data cannot guarantee that predictions remain effective. A change in conditional probabilities also need not change the optimal class for every input. Whether a prediction rule has failed depends on task loss and decision requirements, rather than on a distributional difference alone. The survey concentrates on real drift under online supervision; it is not a comprehensive survey of unlabeled anomaly detection.

## Temporal forms of drift shape the adaptation problem

Distribution change can unfold in several ways. Sudden drift switches from an old concept to a new one over a short period. Incremental drift moves through a sequence of intermediate concepts. Gradual drift can instead consist of old and new concepts alternating during a transition. The first emphasizes continuous movement of the concept; the second emphasizes a mixture of old and new. An experiment must distinguish them. [Accepted manuscript §2.2 and Figure 2, physical pages 5–6](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Recurring concepts create another requirement: a previously unsuitable model can become useful again. Clearing all history at every change means relearning an old concept from scratch; retaining old knowledge indefinitely can weigh down new judgments. A single outlier is also distinct from sustained drift. Figure 2 lists a one-off anomaly separately, showing the tension between reacting quickly and staying stable in noise.

These forms are tools for description and experimental construction; real streams can combine them. Frequency, magnitude, and the affected region matter too. A change confined to a small part of the input space may not warrant replacing the entire model; frequent changes may arrive before the model has learned a stable rule. A claim of adaptation should be understood in terms of the drift form, speed, and feedback conditions under which it was supported.

## Prediction, diagnosis, and updating as composable stages

The authors organize methods into four modules: **memory, learning, loss estimation, and change detection**. These are combinable functions of an adaptive system rather than mutually exclusive algorithm categories. Figure 3 shows the flow of information; Figure 4 expands the modules into a taxonomy. [Accepted manuscript §2.4, §3 and Figures 3–4, physical pages 6–7, 10–11](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

In a direct supervised flow, the current model predicts for $X_t$; when the true label arrives, the system calculates loss and decides whether to use the example for an update. Memory determines which data to retain, learning builds or adjusts the predictive model, loss estimation turns a sequence of outcomes into a comparable signal, and a detector uses that signal to decide whether to raise an alarm. The detector is optional: a system can instead update continuously using recent data without an explicit alarm.

Detection and adaptation answer different questions. An alarm asks whether a monitored signal has changed enough to merit attention; adaptation decides which data, parameters, or models to change next. One detector can be connected to different learning strategies, and one learner can use different forgetting and detection mechanisms. If an experiment replaces the detector while also changing the training window, model capacity, and evaluation window, the source of an improvement becomes hard to identify.

The sequence also has a timing condition: labels can arrive late while new inputs continue to flow. Predicting before diagnosis does not imply immediate access to a label for each sample. Delayed feedback postpones available error evidence; incorrect labels, bias, and the cost of requesting labels also change which strategies are feasible. Section 3.5 explicitly identifies these practical limits. [Accepted manuscript §2.4 and §3.5, physical pages 6–7, 25](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

## What to retain: windows, decay, and old models

The survey separates short-term memory in the form of data from long-term memory in the form of models. This memory serves predictive learning; it is not directly equivalent to an agent's text notes or retrieval database. A single-sample update processes only the current example, yet old patterns can remain accumulated in parameters or statistics. Discarding raw examples therefore does not ensure rapid forgetting of an old concept. [Accepted manuscript §3.1 and Figure 5, physical pages 10–12](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

A sliding window retains a recent batch of examples. A short window reduces the influence of the old concept and usually reacts quickly, but its data may be sparse, unstable, or biased. A long window offers more evidence during stability but mixes in more outdated examples after a switch. The FLORA family illustrates this trade-off; later methods also grow the window during stability and shrink it when change appears. The key assumption is that newer means more relevant. Recurring concepts, noise, and slow changes lasting longer than the window can undermine it. [Accepted manuscript §3.1.1, physical pages 11–12](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Forgetting can be abrupt or gradual. Excluding examples outside a window from training is abrupt forgetting; decaying the weights of older examples or sufficient statistics is gradual forgetting. In the latter case, old statistics can be multiplied by $0<\alpha<1$ before new information is added. The smaller $\alpha$ is, the faster the past loses influence. This need not require physically retaining every historical example: when a learner can be expressed through finite statistics, old information can decay in compressed form. [Accepted manuscript §3.1.2 and Table I, physical pages 12–14](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Sampling serves a different retention goal. Reservoir sampling, for example, uses limited capacity to represent the entire stream observed so far; it does not inherently prioritize the current concept. Windows, weights, and sampling make different assumptions about which history remains valuable, rather than being interchangeable compression tricks. If an old situation can recur, retaining a few models suited to it may be more useful than keeping all raw data, provided the system can reliably recognize the situation and select a model again.

## Detecting change: the monitored signal matters more than the algorithm name

The survey divides explicit detection into sequential analysis, statistical process control, two-part distribution comparison, and contextual methods. All face a trade-off between detection delay and false alarms, but their observations, memory requirements, and assumptions differ. [Accepted manuscript §3.2, Figure 6 and Tables II–III, physical pages 13–19](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

**Sequential analysis** accumulates departures over time into a statistic. For the survey's one-sided CUSUM example, $g_t=\max(0,g_{t-1}+x_t-\delta)$ accumulates positive departures of the monitored signal; an alarm is raised when it exceeds threshold $\lambda$. Here $x_t$ can be a defined scalar signal such as a prediction residual, $\delta$ is a tolerated shift, and $\lambda$ controls how much accumulated evidence is required. The method runs with few stored statistics, but the signal and thresholds affect missed detections, false alarms, and speed. Page–Hinkley likewise accumulates evidence, constructing its detection statistic from departures relative to a mean. [Accepted manuscript §3.2.1, physical pages 14–15](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

**Statistical process control** monitors classification error rate and its uncertainty, distinguishing normal, warning, and out-of-control states relative to a historically low error level. The warning stage can collect new examples before stronger evidence triggers a replacement model. Thresholds depend on statistical modeling and approximations: an out-of-control state is not proof at a fixed probability that the real concept has changed. Label noise and shortcomings of the model can also affect error rates. [Accepted manuscript §3.2.2, physical pages 15–16](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

**Two-part distribution comparison** separates older and newer data or performance signals and tests for differences. ADWIN searches its current window for subwindows with a sufficiently large statistical difference and discards the older part when it finds one. The inputs discussed by the survey have fixed bounds and can be normalized to $[0,1]$; a compressed implementation can use aggregation to reduce storage. This guide explains the mechanism rather than treating the manuscript's threshold expression as a complete reproducible implementation specification. [Accepted manuscript §3.2.3, physical pages 16–18; appendix Algorithm 2, physical page 36](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Finally, **contextual methods** attempt to identify relatively stable situations and learn a corresponding concept. Splice, for example, uses partitions of temporal features to find context intervals. Change need not be represented by a single error threshold, but this approach brings its own assumptions about recognizing context. A detector that observes only inputs, only average error, or only one model component is correspondingly limited in what it can find.

## After detection: update, replace, or combine models

The learning module has three independently selectable dimensions: retraining versus incremental updates; continuous blind adaptation versus signal-triggered informed adaptation; and maintaining one model versus multiple models. Blind adaptation means there is no explicit change alarm, not that the method is careless. Examples include periodic retraining on a fixed window and continuous downweighting of old examples. It is straightforward to run, but still forgets during stable periods and may react too slowly to rapid change. Informed adaptation can accelerate updates in response to alarms, while inheriting the detector's false alarms and delay. [Accepted manuscript §3.3.1–3.3.2 and Figure 7, physical pages 19–21](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Adaptation can affect an entire model or replace only an affected part. CVFDT monitors whether old tree splits remain valid in a recent window and builds candidate subtrees at relevant nodes. When a candidate performs better on new data, it replaces the old part. This illustrates how a judgment can change without clearing the whole structure, although local updates require a structure that can locate and isolate the affected region. [Accepted manuscript §3.3.2, physical pages 21–22](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

An ensemble distributes experience retention and adaptation across multiple models. It can adjust combination weights, update existing members, or add and remove members; these operations can be combined. Dynamic Weighted Majority reduces the weights of experts that make errors on recent data and adds or removes experts according to its management rules. Recurring-concept management can also put old models to sleep and reactivate them when their situations return. Multiple models offer more choices but cost memory, prediction time, and updates; greater complexity alone does not establish that an ensemble is better for long-running operation. [Accepted manuscript §3.3.3 and Table IV, physical pages 22–24](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

## Demonstrating effective adaptation: predict before learning the answer

A system needs internal loss estimates to drive updates, while researchers need independent evaluation to compare complete strategies. Similar statistics can serve these different purposes. Three windows also need distinguishing: the **training window** determines what the model learns from, the **detection window** determines which signals are tested for change, and the **evaluation window** determines which period of performance is displayed. Equal sizes do not make them the same design choice. [Accepted manuscript §3.2.3 footnote, §3.4 and Figure 8, physical pages 17, 23–25](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

For data arriving over time, arbitrary shuffling before cross-validation destroys the real order. Prequential evaluation, or test-then-train, requires the current model to predict and be tested on each example before that example can be used for training. This approximates the information available to the system at the time: what could it predict before knowing the answer? With delayed labels, a natural extension in this guide is to save the original prediction and calculate its loss when the label arrives, rather than letting future answers enter training early. [Accepted manuscript §4.2.1, physical pages 26–27](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Cumulative loss since the start describes the overall experience but can hide a recent failure. A sliding window or decaying weights emphasize recent performance, although too short a window increases variability. The statistical scope and the time curve therefore matter alongside a single overall average. In manuscript Figure 11, a windowed prequential estimate with length 1000 is closer to the holdout curve in that example. This observation does not guarantee that any window and stream can replace an independent test set. [Accepted manuscript §4.2.1; Appendix C and Figure 11, physical pages 27, 43](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

The paper also discusses constrained sequential perturbations and splitting related time streams between tuning and final testing. These check robustness while retaining the relevant temporal structure, rather than permitting arbitrary shuffling. Significance comparisons must match data dependence and the experimental unit; names of tests listed in the survey are not a statistical guarantee that they can be applied directly to every sequence of dependent observations. [Accepted manuscript §4.2.1–4.2.3 and Figure 9, physical pages 27–28](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

## Detection scores, task performance, and resource costs belong together

A complete prediction system can be evaluated with accuracy, recall, or regression error as appropriate to the task, and compared with simple baselines. A detector needs separate measures of detected true changes, false alarms, and delay from the true change to the alarm. Fast detection need not improve the task: frequent false alarms can cause repeated retraining, and an alarm may arrive without enough new labels to support an update. The detector and the subsequent adaptation strategy must be examined separately. [Accepted manuscript §4.1, physical pages 25–26](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Synthetic data can supply known drift locations, types, and speeds, making it useful for measuring detection delay and differences between mechanisms. Real data tests performance in actual environments but often lacks reliable ground truth for drift. Recombining real examples and inserting artificial drift produces “real data with synthetic drift”; it cannot directly establish validation on naturally occurring drift. This distinction matters for experiments with any long-running input stream. [Accepted manuscript §4.3 and Appendix B, physical pages 28–29, 38–41](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

Resources are part of the evidence. Appendix C compares Hoeffding Tree, Adaptive Hoeffding Tree, ADWIN Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging on a Random RBF stream of one million examples. Centers move at $10^{-4}$ per example and the evaluation window is 1000. In Figure 12 the ensemble methods perform better, while Leveraging Bagging uses substantially more RAM-Hours. This unit combines memory use and duration; it is not a universal cost model for present-day devices, power use, or prices. Figure 12 supports a performance–resource trade-off in this particular setting, rather than superiority of ensembles on every drift task. [Accepted manuscript Appendix C and Figure 12, physical pages 43–44](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

## Application conditions and the boundary of the agent connection

The survey illustrates adaptation needs with four application groups. Industrial monitoring signals can change with operating conditions, and true outcomes may be hard to obtain directly. Energy and demand forecasting must account for temporal dynamics. Personal information services such as news recommendation and email filtering face changing interests and sparse feedback. Autonomous systems need effective classifiers as lighting, road conditions, and other circumstances change. The shared task is to identify the source of change, how labels are obtained, and which errors justify adaptation costs, rather than simply attach a drift algorithm. [Accepted manuscript §2.5.1–2.5.4, physical pages 7–10](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

The connection to agents has a boundary. The survey mainly studies online adaptation of predictive models; it provides neither a mechanism for concurrent information injection during large-model generation nor a solution to full belief revision or action planning. As an editorial connection, it helps ask what failure signals a system monitors, whether forgetting harms recurring concepts, and whether evaluation uses future information prematurely. Those questions do not automatically constitute a validated new method.

The paper's conclusion also observes that, when actions influence subsequent data, replaying historical data alone is insufficient to evaluate actual closed-loop behavior. Most surveyed methods depend on timely supervision, while delayed, on-demand, or unsupervised feedback broadens the problem. These are limitations and directions identified by a historical paper, not evidence that nobody has solved them today; offline replay results cannot simply be equated with an agent's effects when acting in a real environment. [Accepted manuscript §5, physical page 29](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

## Reading the source and scope of this guide

For the concepts, start with physical pages 3–7 of the accepted manuscript and Figures 1–3, distinguishing distributions, temporal forms, and the prediction–diagnosis–update sequence. For ways to retain history, continue to pages 11–13 and 22–23 and compare data forgetting with model reuse. For experimental design, focus on pages 25–28 and then the evaluation curves on pages 43–44: which moments of performance are estimated, and what resources produced that performance? [Source entry: accepted manuscript §2–4 and Appendix C, physical pages 3–28, 43–44](https://mpechen.win.tue.nl/publications/pubs/Gama_ACMCS_AdaptationCD_accepted.pdf)

The Chinese guide covers definitions and types of change, the four-module taxonomy, representative detection and adaptation mechanisms, feedback conditions, major evaluation designs, and application limits. Its author examined pixels of the pages containing Figures 1–9 and 11–12 and Tables I–V. Appendix algorithms and datasets received selective checks for mechanisms and evaluation; the author did not examine all pseudocode line by line, verify every dataset and cited study, or reproduce the experiments. Its scope is therefore mainline, rather than a complete substitute for the paper or a panorama of the present field. The primary source was the accepted manuscript; the formal published version and its separate supplements were not compared word for word.
