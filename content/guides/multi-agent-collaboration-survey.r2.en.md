# Multi-Agent Collaboration Survey: Comparing Division of Work Through Collaboration Channels

Splitting one task among several agents driven by large language models became one of the fastest-growing research directions around 2024. Its advocates offer reasons that all sound sensible: knowledge can be stored in different places, long tasks can be broken up, different roles can check one another, and subtasks can run in parallel. But as soon as you try to compare two systems, you hit a problem: each paper describes itself in its own terms. Some talk about architecture, some about roles, some about applications, and the same system carries different labels in different articles.

*Multi-Agent Collaboration Mechanisms: A Survey of LLMs* takes on exactly this problem. Its authors are Khanh-Tung Tran, Dung Dao, Barry O'Sullivan and Hoang D. Nguyen of University College Cork in Ireland, Minh-Duong Nguyen of Pusan National University in Korea, and Quoc-Viet Pham of Trinity College Dublin. The survey proposes no new method. Its contribution is a descriptive framework: it takes the *collaboration channel* as the unit of analysis, breaks a channel down into four attributes (actors, type, structure and strategy), and adds a separate orchestration layer that decides when channels appear and in what order. The authors use this framework to organize existing systems, and add chapters on applications and open problems. [Paper identity and version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06322v1)

The framework's value is that it can be filled in. After reading this guide, you can take any multi-agent paper, fill in the four attributes, ask one more question, "is the workflow fixed before the run, or changed during it?", and say which part of the system the paper actually changes. The framework also explains why conclusions such as "multiple agents beat a single agent" so often contradict one another: many comparisons change the actors, the budget and the orchestration at the same time, without a controlled comparison along any single dimension.

The core of the survey is Figure 2 and the four attributes that follow it. This guide follows the survey's own chapter order. We first see why the authors move from a single model to combinations, then read their formal definitions and the overview in Figure 2. Next come four sections, on collaboration types, strategies, structures and orchestration, each with a representative method: what problem it addresses, how it works, and how it differs from its neighbors. After that come the applications chapter and the open problems, and finally the methods are organized into a table that can be read in both directions. Wherever possible we use the paper's own example: the question-answering system in Figure 1 runs from start to finish.

This guide is pinned to the arXiv v1 version of 10 January 2025. It is a companion to the [multi-agent communication survey](/read/multi-agent-communication-survey/1#u02) in this library: that survey follows how one message enters a decision, while this one asks how a system's division of labor and workflow are arranged. If you want the basic structure of an agent first, read the [agent survey](/read/agent-survey/3#architecture). Abbreviations are marked with a dotted underline on first use; select one to see its expansion and origin.

## Why the authors move from a single model to combinations

The survey begins with three weaknesses of a single large model. The first is hallucination: the model confidently produces content with no basis. The second is the nature of autoregressive generation: it writes only one token ahead at a time. The authors sum up this limitation as "being unable to think slowly", meaning that the model lacks a stage in which it weighs things repeatedly before giving an answer. The third is scaling laws: piling on more parameters and data is becoming ever more expensive. Together, these three points form the background of "scaling up has become expensive". [§1, motivation and contributions](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S1)

The authors' way out is to scale out: rather than build an ever bigger model, let several models share the work. The idea is borrowed from collective intelligence in human societies, and the survey names two sources: the metaphor of a "society of mind", and *theory of mind*, the ability of one agent to infer the intentions and beliefs of others. In a system, one model can play a proposer, another a skeptic, and a third goes off to look things up, with information flowing among them through conversation.

The specific benefits they expect are four. Knowledge can be held by different agents rather than squeezed into one context. Long-horizon planning can be split among agents, so that a problem keeps moving forward over a longer interaction. Combining models with different prompts and personas can generalize to more problems. And handling subtasks in parallel can shorten the time it takes to finish complex tasks. From this the authors argue that multi-agent systems pursue collective intelligence: the combined capability of several agents exceeds the sum of their individual contributions. [§1.1 and §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S2)

Next comes the background chapter's definition of a multi-agent system itself. Its four components are agents, environment, interaction and organization. Agents bring roles, capabilities and knowledge. The environment can be a simulated space or a physical one such as a factory, a road network or a power grid. Interaction takes place through standard agent communication languages. Organization can be an assigned hierarchy of control, or it can grow spontaneously out of individual behavior. The authors list four characteristics. Such systems are flexible and scalable, because agents can be added or removed at any time. They are robust and reliable, because control is distributed and the system keeps running when some components fail. They are self-organizing and coordinated, because division of labor, joint decisions and conflict resolution can arise from emergent rules. And they operate in real time, without waiting for step-by-step human confirmation. The authors also give an economics-style explanation: when a task is broken into small pieces, the processing and energy costs are spread over many agents, so the total cost can be lower than solving the problem with one giant. [§2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S2)

The background chapter then turns to language models. The authors attribute LLM capabilities to emergent abilities that come with scale, such as analogical reasoning and zero-shot transfer, and note that LLMs are becoming the "brain" of agents. At the same time they warn that a single model's abilities are discounted in a multi-agent setting: the complexity of coordination, communication and decision-making all rise, and one erroneous output can set off cascading hallucinations. Here the survey cites two positive examples. MetaGPT writes structured standard operating procedures into its prompts to decompose tasks, and the experiments of Consensus-LLM show that models can negotiate and reach shared goals in dynamic environments. [§2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S2)

The background ends with the concept of *collaborative AI*. The authors stress that cooperation is only one end of a spectrum; the full spectrum also includes competition and coopetition. They add a key judgment: language models were never designed or trained to communicate with one another. This sentence explains why the whole field is still at an early stage: interfaces, protocols and evaluation all have to be built from scratch. The [communication survey](/read/multi-agent-communication-survey/1#u02) in this library deals with that side of the problem: in what format messages travel, in what order, and through whose restatement. [§2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S2)

These sections mark out the scope of the discussion. The four benefits are the authors' expectations for, and summary of, the field, rather than experimental results this survey tests. Later on, the reader will see that the open problems the authors list correspond exactly to the places where these expectations have not yet been met.

In the same chapter the authors also use Table 1 to explain why another survey was needed. Table 1 compares eight related surveys on four criteria: whether they focus on multi-agent collaboration systems, whether they review the aspects and mechanisms of collaboration, whether they propose a general framework, and whether they review real-world applications. A few surveys cover applications broadly, but most score low in the "collaboration mechanisms" column; the authors mark their own row High on all four. The comparison makes the survey's positioning clear, and it is also a reminder that "High" rests on coverage, not on any experimental comparison. [§1.2 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S1.SS2)

## Definitions: an agent, a system, a collaboration channel

Before reviewing methods, the authors use a set of symbols to write a system as an object that can be compared. This section's definitions reward slow reading, because every later paper has to be placed on a channel, and these symbols define what "placed on a channel" means. [§3, concepts and definitions](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S3)

An agent is written $a=\{m,o,e,x,y\}$. Here $m$ is the model, which is itself split into three parts: the architecture $\text{arch}$, the agent's own memory $\text{mem}$, and optional adapters $\text{adp}$. For a language-model agent, the architecture is simply a language model, and its own memory is usually the system prompt $r$. Adapters are pluggable modules; the original text cites speculative decoding and parameter-efficient adapters as examples, whose role is to let this agent connect to other agents' knowledge or capabilities. $o$ is the objective, for example minimizing the cross-entropy between the generated answer and the reference answer in question answering. $e$ is the environment, which for a language model is often a context window limited by the number of tokens. $x$ is the input, encoded as a sequence of tokens. The output $y=m(o,e,x)$ is this agent's response to the input, given its own model, environment and objective.

A system $S$ then contains: a set of agents $\mathcal{A}=\{a_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$, where $n$ can be fixed in advance or adjusted dynamically as needed; a collective objective $\mathcal{O}_{\text{collab}}$, which can be cut into sub-objectives for each agent; a shared environment $\mathcal{E}$, which in a language-model system may be a vector database or a common communication interface; a set of channels $\mathcal{C}=\{c_j\}$; and a system input $x_{\text{collab}}$ and system output $y_{\text{collab}}$. Written as a single line, this structure is Equation 1 of the paper: [Equation 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S3.E1)

$$
y_{\text{collab}}=S(\mathcal{O}_{\text{collab}},\mathcal{E},x_{\text{collab}}\mid\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C})
=\bigl\{c_j\bigl(\{a_i(o_i,\mathcal{E},x_i)\mid a_i,o_i,x_i\in c_j\}\bigr)\mid c_j\in\mathcal{C}\bigr\}
$$

The formula has three layers of nesting, and it is worth reading them one at a time. Innermost, $a_i(o_i,\mathcal{E},x_i)$ is the output one agent produces under the shared environment and its own objective: a sentence, a tool call or an action. One layer out, $c_j(\cdot)$ is a channel: it collects the outputs of all its members and produces the channel's own output. The outermost braces say that the system output is the set of all channel outputs. In other words, the system does not call everyone together for one meeting; it runs several channels at the same time, each with its own attributes and its own members.

A channel is characterized by four attributes. *Actors*: who is in this channel. *Type*: cooperation, competition or coopetition. *Structure*: peer-to-peer, centralized or distributed. *Strategy*: rule-based, role-based or model-based. The authors give a rule for telling channels apart: if any one of the four attributes differs, there are two different channels. Their example is a system with a peer-to-peer structure in which two models compete and two others cooperate; these two groups of interactions belong to two channels. A statement such as "this system is competitive" therefore only becomes precise when it is attached to a channel, because one system can contain competitive and cooperative channels at the same time.

The definition also contains an easily overlooked assumption: the authors assume that agents within a channel share a "common ground", meaning that their interfaces are mutually intelligible, for example that they all use English and are discussing the same topic. This assumption puts "can they understand one another at all" outside the framework. That is deliberate, because it belongs to communication research. But it also means that the framework describes how agents that can already talk are organized, not how they learn to talk.

The framework's usefulness only shows once these symbols are applied to a concrete system. Figure 1 of the paper gives the example of a question-answering system. A user asks a question; a proponent agent makes a claim and asks the other side what it thinks; an opponent agent says it disagrees but needs more information, then queries two research assistants about environmental impact and stability respectively, and finally sends the answer back to the user. [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S1.F1)

![Two channels in a question-answering system: a proponent responds to a user, an opponent disagrees and asks two research assistants about environmental impact and stability, then returns an answer. Orange arrows mark a competitive, rule-based, decentralized channel; green arrows mark a cooperative, role-based, centralized channel.](assets/multi-agent-collaboration-survey/figure-1-two-channels.webp "Original Figure 1: Two collaboration channels in a question-answering system, with different types, strategies and structures")

It helps to read the figure by following the arrows. The user asks on the left, and a black line enters the system and lands on the proponent. An orange arrow points from the proponent to the opponent; the caption labels this channel competitive, rule-based and decentralized, and "rule-based" refers to the turn-taking the caption describes. Green arrows point from the opponent to each of the two research assistants; this channel is labeled cooperative, role-based and centralized, with the opponent as the center: it sends out the queries and also integrates what comes back into the final answer. The user is not a member of either channel, but receives the final answer that runs back from the opponent.

The figure also shows a direct consequence of the framework: the system as a whole has no single type. Between the proponent and the opponent there is competition; between the opponent and the research assistants there is cooperation; and the two channels differ in strategy and structure too. The only way to describe this system is to fill in one channel at a time, which is what the earlier rule, "any differing attribute makes another channel", is for.

Look once more at the question itself. It carries a premise: it asks "why is cryptocurrency more sustainable than fiat currency?" The proponent follows the premise and supplies reasons. The opponent does not go on inventing reasons; it says "I disagree, but I need more information" and then dispatches two queries. This is one reading of Figure 1 offered by the guide: the system gets a chance to test the premise, rather than gathering several more voices to restate an existing claim. What triggers the judgment "more information is needed", and how the research assistants' findings enter the final answer, the figure does not say; those are exactly the parts to look for when reading the original papers.

Finally, there is the question of which stage of the model life cycle collaboration happens at. The authors distinguish three. In the early stage, agents share data, context and environment. In the middle stage, they exchange parameters or weights, with federated learning as the typical case. In the late stage, they aggregate outputs and actions, with ensemble learning as the typical case. All three stages can appear in the same system. This distinction is spelled out on the right side of Figure 2, to which the next section returns.

## Figure 2: four attributes and an orchestration layer

![The collaboration framework: three agents at left each have perception, model, adapters, objective and action; channel types, structures and strategies occupy the center; early, middle and late exchanges appear at right; a green coordination and orchestration arrow points toward the channel area.](assets/multi-agent-collaboration-survey/figure-2-framework.webp "Original Figure 2: The authors' multi-agent collaboration framework")

Figure 2 is the heart of this survey. It turns the symbols of the previous section into a picture that can be checked piece by piece. Read it as three panels from left to right, plus one green arrow cutting across them. [§3 and Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S3)

The left column shows three agents stacked vertically, with an ellipsis between them to indicate that there can be more or fewer. Every agent has the same make-up. The purple Perception is the input, the $x$ in the notation. The yellow AI Model is the model $m$; to its right hang two Adapter boxes and an ellipsis, meaning that several adaptation modules can be attached. Another yellow arrow labeled Objective points into the model from the right and corresponds to the objective $o$. The orange Environment / Context wraps around the model and corresponds to the environment $e$. The red Action is the output, corresponding to $y$. The layout makes a specific point: perception, model, objective, environment and action belong inside the agent, not to the channel.

The large central area is the collaboration channel, and its three rounded boxes correspond to three of the channel's attributes. The Types box uses three icons, a handshake, a trophy and a handover, for cooperation, competition and coopetition. The Structures box uses three icons, radiating, meshed and tree-shaped, for centralized, distributed and hierarchical. The Strategies box uses a rulebook, two figures and two distribution curves for rule-based, role-based and model-based. The three boxes are three parallel axes, not three steps. When reading the figure, keep in mind that any real system takes one value from each box, and the combination of the three makes up its channel attributes.

The right column shows the three exchange stages mentioned in the previous section, from top to bottom early, middle and late, each listing what exactly is exchanged at that stage. The early stage has context sharing, data exchange and input embeddings; the middle stage has model sharing, weight sharing and intelligence exchange; the late stage has output sharing, goal sharing and task sharing. This column answers "at which layer does the exchange happen". The closer it is to the inside of the model, the tighter the bandwidth and privacy constraints, and the harder it is to complete with a single natural-language message. Several members arranged around one hub look like task collaboration when their edges carry text, and are federated training when the edges carry weights.

The green arrow labeled Coordination / Orchestration Layer runs across the center of the figure, from the three agents on the left toward the channel area in the middle. Its position expresses a design choice: orchestration acts on the set of channels, creating channels, arranging their order and declaring dependencies, rather than acting inside a single channel. The three central boxes describe "what one channel looks like"; the arrow describes "when these channels appear and in what order they run".

So the framework has five things to fill in: actors, type, structure, strategy, and orchestration. The abstract and the conclusion both count orchestration as the fifth dimension, where it appears as coordination protocols and coordination mechanisms. Chapter 4 gives it a section of its own: four sections cover the attributes one by one, and a fifth covers how channels are arranged relative to one another. This structural difference is why orchestration is drawn in Figure 2 as an arrow cutting across, not as a fourth box. [§4.6 and §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS6)

| Dimension | What it answers | Common values | What to look for in a paper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Actors | Who is in this channel | Two or more named roles; instances of the same model | Whether members really receive different inputs, tools and objectives |
| Type | How the members' objectives relate | Cooperation, competition, coopetition | Whether objectives are shared, mutually exclusive or partly overlapping |
| Strategy | What constrains the interaction | Rules, roles, models | Who decides the next move |
| Structure | How information and control are distributed | Centralized, decentralized, hierarchical | Who is closest to the information, and who makes the final decision |
| Orchestration | When channels are created and in what order | Static, dynamic | Whether the workflow is fixed before the run or changes during it |

This table can be used directly to read a new paper. Its first row, actors, answers "who is in this channel"; the other four rows correspond to the sections that Chapter 4 of the survey works through one by one. Take the question-answering system in Figure 1 and fill the table in. The actors are the user, the proponent, the opponent and two research assistants. For type there are two channels: competition between the proponent and the opponent, and cooperation between the opponent and the research assistants. Structurally, the first is peer-to-peer and the second uses the opponent as its hub. In strategy, the first follows a turn-taking rule and the second a division of labor by role. For orchestration, whether the system decides in advance when to send the research assistants off to search, or decides on the spot, the figure does not say; that is exactly what to look for in the original paper. [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S1.F1)

What the framework can do is split a system into cells that can be compared separately. What it cannot do is predict which value in which cell gives better results. The survey itself offers no such prediction: it provides a descriptive map, and the method examples in the next four sections are all filling in cells rather than running controlled comparisons. Keeping "this paper falls into a given cell" apart from "this cell works better" avoids many misreadings.

One more detail is worth noticing: the same system is cited in several cells. MetaGPT appears under cooperation, role-based strategy and decentralized structure, because it has a cooperative objective, a division of labor by role and a particular way of handing work over. This is not inconsistency on the authors' part; it is how the framework is meant to be used. A system takes one value on each dimension, and describing it along all of them side by side is more precise than filing it into a single category.

## Types: cooperation, competition and coopetition

The first attribute the authors develop is type, which describes how the members' objectives relate to one another. They use set notation to separate three kinds, and this notation is more useful than adjectives because it forces you to say at which level the objective is written. [§4.2, collaboration types](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS2)

In cooperation, the union of the individual sub-objectives equals the collective objective, $\mathcal{O}_{\text{collab}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}o_{i}$. Members assess one another's needs and abilities, actively look for ways to work together, and each takes the subtasks it is good at. In competition, the objectives differ and conflict with one another, $\mathcal{O}_{\text{collab}}=\{o_{i}\mid o_{i}\neq o_{j},\ \forall i\neq j\}$; debates and strategy games are typical settings. Coopetition mixes the two: cooperating on some tasks and competing on others.

![Examples of collaboration types: an editor, translator, researcher and verifier cooperate on academic writing; defense and prosecution seek opposing outcomes in a courtroom simulation; policy supporters and opponents compromise on some issues and compete on others.](assets/multi-agent-collaboration-survey/figure-3-collaboration-types.webp "Original Figure 3: Cooperation, competition and coopetition; system prompts give agents different tools or capabilities")

Figure 3 draws one scene for each of the three types. [Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.F3)

The left panel shows academic writing support. The editor handles the prose, the translator handles language conversion, the researcher adds material and the verifier checks facts; the handshake icon among the four roles marks that they share one deliverable. The middle panel shows a courtroom simulation: the defense and the prosecution each hold one side, and the two hands in the middle hold a red card and a green card respectively, signaling that their claims exclude each other and the outcome depends on the verdict. The right panel shows policy-making: those who support a policy and those who oppose it compete over specific clauses while also reaching partial agreement with the policymaker; three overlapping hands in the figure suggest both collaboration and exchange.

The difference among the three scenes lies in the objectives. The four roles in the writing system want the same manuscript, and any one of them going astray drags the whole down. The two sides in court want opposite outcomes, so one side's gain is the other's loss. In the policy scene, each party has to give ground on some clauses while fighting for others. In the caption the authors add a key piece of information: each agent gets different tools or capabilities through its system prompt $r$. This sentence links type to strategy: role differences have to be realized through prompts, tools or input material, and a job title alone does not make a division of labor.

Debate is the most studied kind of competition. In the approach of Du et al., several model instances each give an answer and their reasoning, are then shown one another's answers, and after several rounds converge on a common answer. The whole process works with black-box models and uses the same prompts for every task. The original paper reports that this "society of mind" approach improves mathematical and strategic reasoning and reduces wrong answers and hallucinations. Within this survey's framework, the relation between instances is recorded as competition, because in each round they contend over "whose answer becomes the final answer"; at the level of the system, however, both sides serve the same correct answer. The same interaction is recorded as a different type depending on the level at which the objective is written. [Du et al., multi-instance debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325)

LLMArena turns competition into an evaluation environment. It provides seven dynamic game environments and uses TrueSkill ratings to measure spatial reasoning, strategic planning, numerical reasoning, risk assessment, communication, opponent modeling and team collaboration. In tic-tac-toe, for example, the board is placed in the environment as text, and the two agents are told through their system prompts to defeat each other, because their individual objectives are mutually exclusive. The original paper concludes that such environments make it possible to look at skills separately, but that models still lag clearly in opponent modeling and team collaboration, which happen to be the hardest parts of competition and cooperation respectively. [LLMArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16499)

CompeteAI places competition in a setting closer to society. It builds a virtual town with two kinds of agents, restaurants and customers. Two restaurants compete for the same customers; each can see its rival's previous-day menu, customer flow and reviews, and adjusts its business strategy accordingly. The original paper observes shifts in strategy at both the micro and the macro level, and notes that these phenomena are consistent with existing market and sociological theories. The design's value is that it turns "does competition push agents to improve?" into an experiment that can be observed repeatedly, rather than a design slogan. [CompeteAI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17512)

Coopetition gets the least attention in the survey, and the authors note in Table 2 that in-depth research on it is still scarce. They point to two directions: negotiation and mixture of experts. In negotiation simulations, the parties value the same set of issues differently, so each can compete on the clauses it cares about and give ground on minor ones. The mixture-of-experts example combines several expert models through a gate: during training the experts differentiate into their own specialties, and at inference the gate picks experts for each input. Reading MoE as coopetition is an analogy. The experts really do "compete" for the chance to be selected, but they compete through a gating function, with no natural-language channel between them. The analogy is useful as a reminder that collaboration types can occur inside a model, at the cost of a mechanism very different from conversational collaboration.

Mixed channels are the more common reality. When LEGO generates causal explanations, three agents first cooperate to complete the task information, and then an explainer and a critic enter a competitive cycle of iterative improvement: two stretches of channel in one system, with different attributes. The structure of debate plus judge is the same: the two debaters form a competitive channel, the judge and the debaters form a cooperative channel, and the judge's job is to converge on a final verdict. The authors use this example to show that system-level success often comes from combining several channels, not from any single type working alone.

Table 2 lists the three types' definitions, strengths, weaknesses and example scenarios side by side. Cooperation's strengths are assigning work by strength, clear objectives and ease of implementation; its weaknesses are that inconsistent understanding of the objective slows everyone down and a single agent's failure gets amplified. Competition's strengths are pushing members to perform better and encouraging adaptive strategies; its weakness is that mechanisms are needed to keep the competition constructive. Coopetition's strength is reaching mutual benefit through trade-offs; its weakness is that it is still little studied. [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.T2)

The judgment most worth taking away is that a type label describes a relationship between objectives, so before deciding on it, first ask at which level the objectives are written. Competition at the level of a channel can serve cooperation at the level of the system; conversely, a system that claims cooperation everywhere can undermine itself in execution because its objectives are vague. This also explains why the survey files debate under competition while the original debate paper calls it a cooperative society of mind: the two write the objective at different levels.

## Strategies: what determines the next move?

Next comes strategy, which answers what constrains the interaction within a channel: who speaks when, and what decides it. [§4.3, strategies](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS3)

A rule-based strategy controls interaction with rules written in advance, such as fixed rounds, speaking order, voting and majority decisions. What it buys is predictability and traceability: the system's behavior can be traced back to specific rules, which makes it easy to debug. The authors add that rules impose the same constraints on every agent, which helps ensure fairness. The price is poor adaptability: when a situation arises that the rules do not cover, the system either stalls or depends on someone adding a rule by hand, and the number of rules grows quickly with task complexity. The survey gives four examples. One protocol, inspired by social psychology, has agents act out human forms of collaboration such as debate and majority voting. A dynamic, event-triggered rule fires communication only when specific conditions are met, cutting out unnecessary conversation. A protocol inspired by peer review has agents criticize, revise and criticize again in turn. And consensus seeking in multi-robot systems was studied experimentally with four consensus strategies, along with the effect of agent personality and network topology on whether the rules are followed.

A role-based strategy divides work by responsibility: each agent takes one sub-objective of $\mathcal{O}_{\text{collab}}$, usually cut along lines of domain knowledge. AgentVerse is the most frequently cited framework in this direction. It splits a collaboration into four stages, recruiting experts, collaborative decision-making, executing actions and evaluating results, with members handling each stage by role. MetaGPT goes further: it encodes the standard operating procedures of human workflows into a sequence of prompts, so that a product manager, an architect and engineers hand work along a pipeline while downstream roles check the intermediate products. The authors argue that this reduces the logical inconsistencies produced by naively chaining models together. The multi-robot system RoCo has each robot take part in planning in a conversational role, and BabyAGI advances three chains in parallel: task creation, task prioritization and task execution. The benefit of role strategies is modularity and reuse, since each role can be developed, replaced and upgraded on its own. The risk is that the system freezes when roles are defined vaguely, and when the interface between roles gets blocked, overall performance collapses with it. [MetaGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352) · [AgentVerse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10848)

A model-based strategy has agents make probabilistic inferences about the environment, their peers' states or possible outcomes, and decide how to interact on that basis. "Model" here means an estimate of others and of the environment; it does not refer to "using a language model". The survey's examples include using theory of mind to infer a peer's beliefs and adjust one's own share of the work accordingly; treating logical rules as latent variables and combining hierarchical reinforcement learning with theory of mind to infer human goals, with gains on the Watch-and-Help and HandMeThat benchmarks; using probabilistic graphical models to infer other agents' goals and degree of rationality, in order to handle ambiguous context; and using probabilistic timed automata to model state transitions so that channels change with time and location, as in an adaptive parking system. Such strategies work best in dynamic, uncertain environments. The price is complex implementation, high computational cost, and the fact that a wrong inference can go on being wrong without anyone noticing.

Table 3 compares the three side by side. Rule-based strategies are efficient, predictable and consistent, but adapt poorly and are hard to extend to complex tasks. Role-based strategies are modular and make use of each member's specialty, but their structure is rigid and performance depends on the quality of the connections between roles. Model-based strategies adapt to dynamic environments and are robust to uncertainty, but are complex to implement and computationally expensive. [Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.T3)

In practice the three strategies are often layered. MetaGPT's pipeline is both a division of labor by role and a set of hard-coded handover rules. A debate system that takes turns by rounds, if one agent estimates before each round how the other side will rebut, is using rules and a model at the same time. So when a paper says "our system uses role-based division of labor", it is worth asking one more question: what actually decides who speaks next? If the answer is "roles take turns in a fixed order", then it is rules doing the work, and the roles only determine how the content is divided.

Going back to the example in Figure 1 makes the difference among the three clear. If the proponent and the opponent alternate in fixed rounds and a judge votes at the end, that is a rule strategy. If the system assigns work as "the research assistants verify, the opponent integrates", that is a role strategy. If the opponent, before speaking, estimates the user's real intention and whether the other side would accept a given premise, and then decides whether to ask for more material, that moves into a model strategy. The last case is a projection the guide sets up for illustration; Figure 1 does not draw any such inference process.

## Structures: where information and responsibility converge

The third attribute developed is structure, which describes who is connected to whom and where control is concentrated. In this section the survey divides structures into three groups, centralized, decentralized or distributed, and hierarchical, and develops them in three subsections. The opening of the section speaks of "four kinds", which does not match the three items and three subsections that follow; it is easiest to read by subsection. [§4.4, structures](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS4)

![Three communication structures: centralized examples include federated distributed training, a star with an aggregator, and an LLM-equipped aggregator; decentralized user and item agents exchange feedback; a hierarchical CAMEL diagram shows starting points, options and critic selection.](assets/multi-agent-collaboration-survey/figure-5-communication-structures.webp "Original Figure 5: Centralized, decentralized and hierarchical structures and representative systems")

Figure 5 is this section's classification diagram, and its three parts are worth looking at separately. [Figure 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.F5)

A centralized structure, also called a star structure, connects every agent to one central agent that manages, controls and coordinates. The authors split it into two kinds. In the first, the language models sit on the distributed agents and the center only aggregates, with federated learning as the representative case. The left side of Figure 5(a) shows several local agents, each with a model and adapters, sending updates to a server for aggregation, with FedIT labeled as an example; the authors also mention Google using federated learning to train next-word prediction for a phone keyboard. In the second, the language model sits on the central agent. The Type 2 diagram on the right of Figure 5(a) shows Agent 1 through Agent N feeding a central aggregator that contains a language model, with AutoAct labeled as an example. On the task-execution side, the representative is LLM-Blender: it has different models each answer once, first uses a pairwise-comparison ranker to judge which answers are better, and then fuses the top-ranked candidates into one output. AgentCoord turns the design of coordination strategies into a visual interface, letting users draw how several language models collaborate, and the authors use a user study to show its usability. [LLM-Blender](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02561)

A decentralized structure has no central node: each agent decides on the basis of local information and limited communication. The left side of Figure 5(b) shows a loop of language feedback and preference propagation, and the right side shows several language models connected in pairs and responding to one another, with AgentCF labeled as an example. AgentCF treats both users and items as agents, each with a memory module that stores simulated preferences; at each step, user agents and item agents interact autonomously, and the question is whether these interactions match real behavior records. This subsection lists many other systems. MedAgent combines agents from different medical specialties to analyze a case. ChatDev and MetaGPT have product managers, designers and programmers collaborate by role. AutoAgents and OKR-Agent handle creative writing and storyboard design. SOA extends large codebases in a self-organizing way. ProAgent has agents understand their teammates' intentions and adjust their behavior accordingly. Generative agents use memory, reflection and retrieval to support the daily behavior of a society of agents. OpenAgents provides three agents for data analysis, plugins and web browsing. The survey also mentions that having several instances debate for a fixed number of rounds improves factuality and reasoning, and that some work uses dynamic directed acyclic graphs to organize communication among language models. [§4.4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS4)

A hierarchical structure distributes functions across levels, and each level's agents interact mainly with the same or adjacent levels. The right side of Figure 5(c) shows CAMEL's flow: several options branch out from a starting point, a critic selects among them, and an assistant then expands the next batch of options, moving forward level by level. This section's representative method is DyLAN. It organizes agents into a multi-layer feed-forward network and runs in two stages. The first stage, called team optimization, uses an unsupervised importance score to pick the most contributive agents from the candidates. The second stage, called task solving, has the selected agents collaborate dynamically on the problem, with an LLM-driven ranker that gradually deactivates poorly performing agents, combined with early stopping. The original paper reports that on some MMLU subjects, the team-optimization step alone raises accuracy by up to 25.0 percentage points. AgentVerse is cited in this section for its hydrogen-storage-station siting example, in which agents with different backgrounds discuss in levels and the proposal passes from level to level. [DyLAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02170)

Exchange-of-Thought offers another way to describe structure. Taking its inspiration from network topology, it defines four communication paradigms, memory, report, relay and debate, corresponding to bus, star, ring and tree connections respectively. The correspondence is useful because it links "structure" to "how messages travel": with the same group of agents, switching paradigms changes both the paths and the number of messages. The original paper also adds a confidence-evaluation mechanism to keep faulty reasoning chains from spreading. [Exchange-of-Thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01823)

Table 4 lists the strengths and weaknesses of the three. Centralized structures are simple to design and allocate resources efficiently, but a failure of the central node drags down the whole system, and they resist interference poorly. Decentralized structures keep running when some members fail, scale well and give individuals autonomy, but allocate resources less efficiently and carry high communication overhead. Hierarchical structures spread tasks across levels, with few bottlenecks and efficient resource use, but edge devices become critical points, and complexity and latency are both high.

Figure 5 hides one concrete trap. Both centralized examples look like stars in the figure, but the edges on the left carry model updates while those on the right carry task messages; in the decentralized case, AgentCF's edges carry language feedback and preferences. "One edge" means something entirely different in the three cases. So when comparing communication costs, first ask what an edge carries and how much one exchange amounts to, before saying whose overhead is larger. Using the number of edges as the amount of communication would put one round of federated parameter aggregation on the same footing as one sentence of conversation.

This section also reveals something about how the framework is used. MetaGPT and ChatDev appear in the decentralized subsection, yet they are organized as role pipelines, and readers can easily assume they are hierarchical. The authors classify by whether control is concentrated, not by whether there is a fixed order of roles. The subsection a system is placed in describes its value on the structure dimension; its values on the other dimensions have to be looked up in the corresponding subsections.

## Orchestration: who creates channels, and when?

Type, strategy and structure describe what one channel looks like. The orchestration layer describes how the set of channels is arranged: which channels exist, in what order they run, and who declares their dependencies. The survey treats this layer separately and splits it into two kinds, static and dynamic. [§4.5, orchestration](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS5)

Static orchestration builds channels from domain knowledge and preset rules, so the workflow is fixed before the run. The most common form is a sequential chain: the previous agent's output, its own intermediate results and the original user input are concatenated and passed to the next agent, written $y_{i+1}=y_{i}\Vert x_{i}\Vert x_{\text{collab}}$, where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation. CoMM chains three agents in this form: the first two are domain experts offering complementary perspectives, and the third summarizes. The authors report that this arrangement beats single-agent chain-of-thought prompting on college-level science multiple-choice questions. [CoMM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17729)

Another idea in static orchestration is to make the workflow mimic a domain's working process. MapCoder splits the program-synthesis loop among four agents, which recall relevant examples, plan, write code and debug respectively, and is evaluated on eight competitive-programming and program-synthesis benchmarks; the original paper reports 93.9% pass@1 on HumanEval. [MapCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11403) MACRec splits recommendation tasks among a manager, user and item analysts, a reflector, a searcher and a task interpreter, with different tasks following different workflows. A literary-translation system makes its channels correspond to the roles in a publishing process. [MACRec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15235) What these systems have in common is that their workflow comes from domain knowledge, and the authors trust that the workflow itself is right.

Dynamic orchestration hands the decision to run time. SPP is a clear example. Given an input, the system first identifies which personas the task needs, and then a manager agent generates agents with customized system prompts, which collaborate with themselves repeatedly around the problem. One finding of the original paper is worth remembering: this cognitive synergy appears only with GPT-4, and not with GPT-3.5 or Llama2-13b-chat. The same orchestration strategy vanishes when the base model changes, which shows that whether dynamically generated roles bring a benefit depends on whether the model itself can play a role reliably. [SPP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05300)

Another example is graph-based orchestration. An orchestrator agent builds a directed acyclic graph from the user input, with tasks as nodes and dependencies and channels as edges; agents execute the tasks in parallel or in sequence according to the graph, and finally a delegator agent gathers the results of all the tasks. Compared with a sequential chain, this arrangement can express parallelism and dependency, at the cost of needing an orchestrator that decomposes tasks correctly, whose errors of judgment turn directly into structural errors. [§4.5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS5)

Table 5 compares the two side by side. Static orchestration guarantees consistent execution and makes full use of domain knowledge, but depends on the initial design and domain knowledge being accurate, and fixed channels lose out on scale and flexibility. Dynamic orchestration can adjust roles and channels to the task and suits complex, changing tasks, at the cost of higher resource consumption from real-time adjustment, and the adjustment itself can fail. [Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS5)

The example in Figure 1 makes the difference between the two clear. A static version might hard-code into the system prompt: debate for three rounds, then send two research assistants to look up one direction each, and finally summarize. A dynamic version needs a judgment step: when the opponent says "I disagree, but I need more information", the orchestration layer decides whether to add a research channel, which direction to investigate, and whether to return to the debate afterwards. The second approach fits the actual needs of the question more closely, and it also makes "who decides whether there is enough information" a key component of the system. This paragraph is a projection by the guide to illustrate the mechanism; the survey reports no such comparison.

The orchestration layer explains an easily overlooked fact about the framework: two systems can have exactly the same four attributes and still perform very differently, and the difference may come from "when channels are created". It is also the hardest column of the framework to read out of a paper, because many papers describe roles and structure in the main text while burying the workflow in implementation code or in prompts in an appendix.

## Six lessons from the authors

Chapter 4 ends with a summary in which the authors set out six recommendations to take away. Reading them after the individual attributes makes them much easier to follow. [§4.6, summary](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S4.SS6)

First, collaboration channels need clear protocols. The authors say that clear protocols prevent misunderstandings and keep information exchange efficient, and they use AutoGen to illustrate how well-designed collaboration mechanisms can let a multi-agent system outperform a single agent. Second, domain knowledge should be brought into the design, which is why many systems fix their channels in advance. Third, roles and channels should be allocated adaptively according to members' strengths and the task's needs; dynamic allocation makes a system better able to cope with changes in its environment. Fourth, the strategy should match the nature of the task: rule-based for tasks that require strict adherence to procedure, role-based for tasks that need specialized division of labor, and model-based for uncertain or dynamic tasks. Fifth, scalability needs separate attention, because coordination itself becomes a bottleneck as the number of members grows. Sixth, ethics and safety should be built into the design, to keep agents from harmful behavior.

In the same paragraph as the first two points, the authors also give a counterexample. One study compared many reasoning tasks and several base models and found that multi-agent systems with poorly designed competitive channels are overtaken by a single agent with a well-written prompt, including relevant few-shot examples. Put the positive example and the counterexample side by side and the paragraph's meaning is clear: the quality of the collaboration design sets the direction, and "more agents" in itself has no direction. The same authors present scaling out as the way forward in Chapter 1 and warn in the summary that it may lose to one carefully prompted model; only the two passages together make up their full judgment.

These six points are a synthesis of the cited work. Most of them can be traced to specific cited papers, but they have not themselves been tested in a common controlled experiment. The fourth is the most closely tied to this chapter and also the easiest to misuse: it says that strategy should match the task, not that any one strategy is better. A system that debates in fixed rounds looks clumsy when a question calls for on-the-spot verification, while a system that relies entirely on the model to decide each next step wastes money on tasks with a clear procedure. In the same paragraph the authors also mention that existing benchmarks measure success rate, task outcomes, cost-effectiveness and collaboration efficiency, which prepares the ground for the later discussion of evaluation.

## Applications across three domains

Chapter 5 of the survey groups systems into three domains: 5G/6G and Industry 5.0, question answering and natural language generation, and society and culture. This chapter is organized by domain rather than by the framework's four attributes, so it calls for a different way of reading than Chapter 4: first see what engineering problem a domain needs to solve, then see which collaboration arrangement a system uses. [§5, applications](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S5)

In communications and industry, the core problems are bandwidth and semantics. LLM-SC has a language model build a probabilistic model of the transmitted text sequence, replacing part of the bit-level coding with semantic encoding and decoding. LaMoSC brings in multimodal fusion, using a language model to generate prompt text and then attention to merge text with visual features. LAM-MSC uses a multimodal language model for modality alignment, so that one codec handles several kinds of data. GMAC uses a language model within a multi-agent system to align observed states with natural language, then compresses them before transmission to reduce communication volume. Figure 6 shows the typical structure of such systems: the upper half is the multi-agent training stage, in which Agent 1 through Agent N exchange data with a central agent over a wireless channel, and the lower half is the semantic communication stage, with the language model attached to the semantic encoder and decoder. On the Industry 5.0 side, the common thread is localization and privacy. One system deploys an open-source model on a local network with prompt-management and post-processing modules; SAGE uses a dynamically constructed prompt tree to decide the next action, assess whether an action succeeded and judge when to stop; LSGLLM attaches spatio-temporal modules to a language model to handle road-network data; and CASIT uses several language models for data analysis and anomaly detection. [Figure 6 and §5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S5)

In question answering and generation, the survey introduces four established frameworks. OpenAI's Swarm organizes multiple agents with two concepts, routines and handoffs: each agent carries instructions and tools and can pass an ongoing conversation to another agent, and that transfer is a handoff. Figure 7 illustrates the mechanism with a customer-service split between sales and refunds. Microsoft's Magentic-One centers on an orchestrator agent responsible for high-level planning, progress tracking and replanning after errors, and dispatches tasks such as browsing the web, examining local files, and writing and running Python to specialized agents. IBM's Bee Agent Framework provides modular components for agents, tools, memory and instrumentation; one concrete capability is serializing agent state so that complex workflows can be paused and resumed. LangChain's agent components provide tools and integrations that let a model work in a loop of reading the environment and making decisions. [§5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S5)

This area also contains two kinds of work that are less conspicuous but very practical. The first is evaluation. Agent-as-a-Judge uses a multi-agent system to evaluate another agentic system, collecting process feedback through a division of labor by role; on the DevAI benchmark of 55 real development tasks, the authors report that it comes closer to human experts than conventional model-based judging. Benchmark Self-Evolving uses multiple agents to rewrite the contexts and questions of existing benchmarks, producing harder new items to counter data contamination. The second is data synthesis. Orca-AgentInstruct generates training data through three flows, content transformation, seed-instruction generation and instruction refinement, each run internally by decentralized agents; the authors use it to fine-tune Mistral 7B and report gains of up to 54% on several benchmarks. [§5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S5)

In society and culture, the emphasis is on simulation. Figure 8 divides applications into three layers: social applications, cultural applications and agent analysis. On the social side, some research discusses using language-model agents to replace part of the human participants in studies; SocialMind combines verbal, nonverbal and social cues to give real-time suggestions through augmented-reality glasses; and some work brings multi-agent systems into agent-based modeling to test policy interventions and detect norm violations. On the cultural side, CulturePark has agents from different cultural backgrounds talk with one another, Mango extracts cultural knowledge from agents, and other studies simulate how culture evolves in a population of agents. The authors also cite contrary evidence: in settings with information asymmetry that require competition and conflict resolution, replacing human participants with language models introduces systematic biases, so standardized evaluation is needed to judge how credible these simulations are. [§5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S5)

The end of this chapter contains an observation well worth remembering: existing frameworks concentrate on role-based strategies and centralized or decentralized structures. In the framework's language, the other cells are still largely empty: coopetition, model-based strategies, and combinations of hierarchy and dynamic orchestration all lack mature engineering practice. This sentence says more about the field's stage of development than any list of applications.

When reading the applications chapter, it is best to add one step yourself: fill in one system along the five dimensions of Figure 2. The survey is organized by domain and does not take this step for you, yet it is exactly what you need to judge whether a system's success comes from its collaboration mechanism or from the characteristics of its domain.

## Open problems: evaluation, governance and safety

The final chapter returns to problems at the level of the field. The authors first discuss five obstacles on the way to artificial collective intelligence, and then discuss evaluation and ethics separately. [§6, open problems](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S6)

The first obstacle is unified governance: who decides which step to take, who takes part, and how tasks are allocated. The authors point out that whether role assignment is optimal and whether agents can adapt to changing tasks are both still open; systems also need to detect and recover from communication failures and task interruptions, for which redundant and standby agents are possible means. The second is shared decision-making. The decision methods current systems commonly use are dictatorial or majority voting, both of which struggle to express the different facets of preferences and may compound the overconfidence of several models. The third is "agents as a digital species". Language models were never designed for collaboration among many participants; hallucinations and adversarial attacks both spread along channels, and one agent's error gets restated and reinforced by others, so small mistakes turn into cascading consequences. The fourth is scalability and resource maintenance: as the number of agents grows, memory, time and coordination channels all become harder to manage, and scaling laws for multi-agent systems have not yet been established. The fifth is unexpected generalization: collective intelligence may give rise to coordinated problem-solving and innovative behavior, but what conditions produce such behavior, and how to make it appear in unseen domains, cannot yet be explained.

Evaluation is where the authors write the most, and it is also the most useful part when reading papers. They point out that evaluating multi-agent systems is more complex than evaluating single models. It requires looking at overall task performance, such as reasoning ability and task completion rate; at system properties, such as coordination efficiency and contextual appropriateness; and at fine-grained diagnosis at the agent and collaboration levels, to locate which member and which channel a problem actually comes from. In reality, most evaluations are done in narrow settings with different configurations, so their results are neither consistent nor comparable; static benchmarks also go stale over time, bringing data leakage and overfitting. The authors therefore call for unified, dynamic benchmarks. [§6.2, evaluation](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S6.SS2)

The passage on ethics and safety sets out a clear mechanism. Language models hallucinate on their own, and once they are placed in a multi-agent system, overconfidence and mutual misunderstanding amplify errors along the channels. These systems also easily become targets of adversarial attacks: a compromised agent can be manipulated into harmful behavior. As the number of agents grows, these risks grow proportionally. The authors also warn that such systems can simulate human society and display human-like psychological traits; once readers attribute human qualities to them, overreliance becomes likely and manipulation becomes harder to detect. [§6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06322v1#S6)

The direct use of this material for reading papers is two sets of checking questions. The first concerns budget: compared with the single-agent baseline, did this system get more model calls, longer contexts or more chances to use tools? If the budgets differ, an end-to-end lead can at first only be credited to the system as a whole. The second concerns attribution: when a role, a particular message or an orchestration step is removed, does the change in performance identify where the gain comes from? These two sets of questions are reading suggestions the guide has assembled from the survey's discussion of evaluation; the survey itself proposes no common way of measuring budgets.

The table also works in the other direction: start from a symptom and find which dimension to check.

| Symptom | Dimension to check first | How to test it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discussion runs a fixed number of rounds and completes every step even in new situations | Orchestration (static) | Use a batch of inputs that need different steps and see whether the workflow changes |
| After several rounds everyone agrees, but on the same mistaken premise | Type and evaluation | Check who makes the final judgment and whether dissent is preserved |
| All the role names are present, yet the outputs are highly repetitive | Strategy and actors | Check whether members' inputs, tools and prompts really differ |
| The system becomes noticeably slower and costs multiply | Structure and orchestration | First establish what one exchange carries, then compare the number of channels |

When reading a new multi-agent paper, you can go through it in this order. First, see what failure it claims to fix. Next, fill in the four attributes and see which one the change falls on. Then ask whether its orchestration is static or dynamic. Finally, check whether the experiments compare against a single agent or other forms of collaboration under the same budget. You can start this sequence without reading the whole paper; the abstract, the method figure and the experimental setup usually already give most of the information.
