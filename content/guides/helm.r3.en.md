# HELM: How a Model Score Is Made

When a paper says a language model is "more accurate", what does that sentence actually tell us? If the task is multiple choice, the score depends on where the items come from, what prompt the model saw, how the candidate answers were presented and how answers were scored. Under a different setup, the same model's performance can change a great deal. Looking only at the final number, it is easy to overlook all of these choices.

*Holistic Evaluation of Language Models* (HELM), by Percy Liang, Rishi Bommasani and colleagues, puts these choices at the center of evaluation. The authors first lay out the scenarios models may face and the requirements we would like them to meet, then select a feasible subset and test 30 models in as uniform a way as possible. They measure accuracy, and alongside it calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency, trying to place the different strengths and risks of models on the same map. [Abstract and §1.1, PDF pp. 1–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=1)

The value of this paper lies in learning to trace a score, not in remembering a historical leaderboard: **what are the scenario and the examples, how was the model adapted, what does the metric measure, and which conditions does the final comparison aggregate?** The guide follows this route. It first uses one of the paper's multiple-choice items to understand the evaluation interface, then compares two kinds of adaptation with Figure 7, works through calibration by hand with Figure 17, and finally looks at the multi-metric results and the boundaries of the evaluation. The paper is pinned to [arXiv 2211.09110v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2); it reflects the models and tasks of its time, not a complete picture of what models can do today.

## Each was tested, so why are they hard to compare?

Suppose two research teams both report "accuracy". One has the model output a letter directly from the question and four options; the other attaches each of the four candidates to the question separately and compares the model's probability for each. Even with the same items, the input the model receives and the action it has to perform are different. Add that one team provides examples and the other does not, and the two numbers are no longer the result of differences between models alone.

Think of a model test as an exam paper plus a set of exam rules. The paper decides what is tested, the rules decide what material the candidate can see and in what format they answer, and the marking method decides what counts as correct. Before placing the scores of two exams side by side, we at least need to know whether the papers and rules were the same; if they differ, the scores can still be reported separately, but they answer different questions.

Evaluating language models adds one more layer to this analogy: the models themselves have different training histories, and some have public parameters while others can be reached only through an interface. Even given the same item, whether an interface can return candidate probabilities affects which evaluation methods are possible. HELM aims to establish a common basis for comparison under these practical constraints, while making its limitations public.

Surveying past evaluations, the authors found that the datasets on which different models had been tested overlapped very little, and some models had no dataset in common when first published. Even when someone later tested different models on the same data, the conditions of fine-tuning, zero-shot prompting or few-shot prompting might differ. HELM therefore divides evaluation into three connected goals: broaden scenario coverage and make omissions explicit, measure as many requirements as possible within the same scenario, and apply a consistent adaptation protocol across many models. [§1, Figures 2–4, PDF pp. 2–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=2)

There are two kinds of coverage here that are easy to confuse. Having chosen 16 core scenarios, the authors looked back at the published evaluations of 30 models in these scenarios and found an average coverage of 17.9%; HELM's unified testing raised it to 96.0%. Both figures count **model–core-scenario pairs**: how many of the 16 scenarios have usable results for each model. The earlier results also pooled different papers and different test conditions. The remaining 4% relates to the interfaces of particular models or to technical problems, for example when the required probabilities are unavailable. [Figure 4 and footnote 5, PDF p. 5; §6, PDF p. 45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=5)

Later we will meet another coverage figure, 87.5%, which counts "scenario–metric category" combinations and has nothing to do with whether a model took part in a scenario. With different denominators, the two answer different questions even though both are called coverage. The former is about whether models have been tested in common; the latter is about whether different requirements are measured together in the same scenario.

"Holistic" also has a specific meaning. The authors first draw a space of tasks, languages and metrics larger than what is actually tested, then select a feasible subset. The regions not selected remain on the map, reminding readers which needs have not yet been adequately evaluated.

How does this design differ from simply collecting a batch of datasets? Suppose a benchmark lists only two datasets, one for question answering and one for summarization. Readers can tell what was tested, but from the list it is hard to see what was not. HELM first treats task, domain and language as the three dimensions of a scenario: the task says what is to be done, the domain describes the text and its background, and the language says which language or language variety is used. Once instances are chosen, readers can see which regions are represented and which are still blank. [§3.1–3.2, PDF pp. 16–19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=16)

Metrics are handled in a similar way. The authors first consider the various requirements a model may need to meet, then select the measurable part in light of how models can be accessed and the cost of implementation. This classification reflects the authors' organization of the evaluation space, and the specific choices also reflect judgments about resources and values. To see how an evaluation actually works, start with a real item from the paper.

## The evaluation interface through one item

Figure 6 of the paper gives an anatomy item from MMLU: which term describes the body's ability to maintain its normal state? The four candidates are Anabolism, Catabolism, Tolerance and Homeostasis, and the reference marks Homeostasis as correct. Understanding the evaluation process does not require knowing anatomy first; this item lets us see that an example consists of an input, candidate references and a correctness mark. [Figures 5–6 and §2.1, PDF p. 14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=14)

In HELM's terminology, a **scenario** describes a use we want the model to serve, made concrete by a set of instances. The MMLU multiple-choice item is one instance in a scenario; sentiment classification or summarization would have different inputs and references. A scenario can have training instances and test instances: the former can be used as examples in the prompt, and the latter check the model's performance on new inputs. "Training instance" here describes a data split; when such instances are placed in the prompt, the model's parameters stay unchanged.

Next, the **model and adaptation** have to be specified. A language model naturally takes text and continues it, while a multiple-choice item requires giving one answer from a limited set of candidates. Adaptation connects these two interfaces: how the question, examples, candidates and instructions are organized into a prompt, how the model is asked to return a result, and how a prediction is obtained from that result. The paper treats models as text interfaces, permitting access to generated text and log probabilities, without requiring internal activations or training data. [§2.2, PDF p. 14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=14)

Finally come the **metrics**. If the system chose D, it can be compared with the reference mark and the item recorded as correct; aggregated over many items, this gives an accuracy metric. Under a different requirement, one could instead observe whether the probability the model assigns to its predictions matches how often it is correct, or whether the result stays stable when the input is changed slightly. One example can thus support different questions, each with its own evaluation rule.

The chain can be written as: the scenario provides examples, adaptation turns examples into model requests, the model returns outputs or probabilities, and the metrics process these results. Figure 5 of the paper lists the model as a separate evaluation component too. Figure 7 then shows that even for exactly the same homeostasis item, there is more than one way to adapt. [Figure 7 and §2.3, PDF p. 15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=15)

Putting the four components back onto the same item shows what each controls. Changing the model while keeping the other steps gives a comparison of models under this protocol; changing the adaptation while keeping the model and examples reveals that model's sensitivity to the interface; changing the metric may yield a different understanding from the same predictions. Every comparison needs to record what was actually changed, or the change in numbers cannot be traced to a definite operation.

Scenarios also have a layer of data source. Whether the questions come from a set of exam items or from search queries changes the language and background the model has to face; whether the reference answer is a letter or a paragraph of summary decides how the output can be scored. HELM describes these choices through task, domain and language, so "question answering" is not one fully homogeneous scenario. Even when both are called question answering, in the open-book setting the model can see given material, while in the closed-book setting it has to answer from the prompt and its existing parameters alone. [§3.1, §3.3, PDF pp. 16, 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=16)

Adaptation is not a shell independent of the data either. The candidate answers of a multiple-choice item already exist in the example's references, but the authors can choose to show all options at once or one at a time. How the system uses these references decides what problem the model actually faces. Figure 7, which we look at next, develops exactly this point.

These components also make errors locatable. The model may lack knowledge of homeostasis, or it may understand the question but fail to output a letter as required; another scoring program might also treat the output differently. All of these end up as an item that did not score, but explaining or fixing it requires distinguishing among the failures. Evaluation records keep the prompts and predictions precisely so that results can be traced back to concrete behavior.

## Figure 7: Two different paths to an answer

![Figure 7 compares two multiple-choice adaptations. At left an answered pleura example has answer C; after an ellipsis, the homeostasis question lists A Anabolism, B Catabolism, C Tolerance and D Homeostasis, and the model generates D with log probability −0.26. Temperature is 0 and the maximum is one generated token. At right the question is paired separately with candidates, showing Anabolism with log probability −0.007 and Homeostasis with −0.005, with other candidates omitted; temperature is 0 and the maximum is zero generated tokens.](assets/helm/figure-7-adaptation.webp "Original Figure 7: Joint adaptation at left and separate adaptation at right")

Start on the left. The prompt first says that this is an anatomy multiple-choice item, then gives an answered example. Next come the homeostasis item and its four options A to D, and the prompt ends with "Answer:". The model has to continue with an answer letter; the completion shown in the figure is D. Temperature is set to 0 and the maximum generation length is 1 token. The question and all candidates appear together in a single request, which is why the authors call this **joint adaptation**. [Figure 7, PDF p. 15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=15)

The example on the left lets the model see the answer format from context: first the question and options, then the correct letter. This is called in-context learning; the example is supplied as input text, and the model's parameters are not updated by the request. The ellipsis in the figure marks content that is not shown, so one cannot infer from the single drawn example that the whole experiment used only one example.

Now look at the right. Instead of placing the four options in one request, the system concatenates the question with each candidate text separately. The figure draws two candidates, Anabolism and Homeostasis, with the others omitted in between. The system reads the log probability the model assigns to the candidate text and then compares the candidates; the maximum number of generated tokens shown is 0, because the point is to score existing text, not to continue with an answer letter. Each candidate needs its own request, which is **separate adaptation**. [§7, PDF p. 47](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=47)

A log probability is simply a probability expressed on a logarithmic scale; under a comparable scoring rule, a larger log probability corresponds to a larger probability. The values in the figure serve to illustrate the interface, and the caption does not say which model or which run they come from, so they should be read as illustrative values, not cited as measured results for some model. The letter generation on the left and the scoring of candidate text on the right also answer different operational questions: the former asks which label the model would write, and the latter asks which piece of candidate text the model scores higher.

The separate method also has a **calibrated variant**. Candidate texts may differ in how common they are in themselves, so the authors also consider the probability the model assigns to a candidate without the question, and use it to correct the score with the question. §7, §8.2 and Appendix J.1 of the paper only outline the role of these two quantities and give no formula or normalization condition specific enough to recompute here; this is a conceptual description, not a complete scoring recipe. It sits at a different stage from the "calibration error" discussed later: the former changes how an answer is chosen, while the latter evaluates the resulting probabilities. [§7, PDF p. 47; Appendix J.1, PDF p. 160](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=160)

Why do these interface choices matter? Because the model answers the actual prompt; it has no direct access to the abstract label "correct option" in the dataset. The joint method supplies all candidates at once, and the separate method supplies one candidate each time; the two may also differ in examples and in request cost. Using one of them uniformly lets different models face the same set of rules, but it does not guarantee that this set of rules suits every model equally well.

In the joint method, the relation between labels and options is established by the prompt: the model has to map "D" to Homeostasis. The separate method directly compares the fit between the candidate text and the question, and the system, not the model, produces the letter. This changes the intermediate steps the model has to perform, and also what has to be obtained from the interface: an interface that provides only generated text may not support scoring candidates by probability.

The two paths also differ in computational cost. One request in the joint method has to hold all the options and any examples; the separate method usually needs a separate query for each candidate. The authors found that which method runs inference faster varies by model, and the length of each request has to be considered as well. Adaptation is both a measurement design and a running design. [§8.2, PDF p. 58](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=58)

Although the two paths send different requests, both end up producing one candidate that can be checked against the same correctness mark. The separate and separate-calibrated methods, however, use different scoring rules, and when looking at the results later they should be kept as two settings. The paper goes on to show the effects of these choices experimentally. [§7, §8.2, PDF pp. 47, 58](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=47)

## The same model: Why prompts change the result

HellaSwag is a commonsense scenario about choosing what happens next: the model reads a situation and has to pick a suitable continuation from candidate endings. The authors compare adaptation methods in this scenario. For OPT 175B, the setting that presents candidates separately with no in-context examples reaches 79.1% accuracy; the setting that presents candidates jointly with five examples reaches 30.2%. That is a very large difference for the same model in the same scenario. [§1.2 finding 23, PDF p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=9)

How should the difference be explained? At least two things change at once here: whether candidates are presented jointly or separately, and whether the number of examples is zero or five. This pair of numbers therefore shows that the complete evaluation protocol significantly affects results, but it cannot by itself prove that "giving examples causes the drop", nor can it attribute the whole gap to how the candidates are organized. Separating either effect would require a controlled comparison with the other conditions fixed.

In Figure 33 the paper also reports an accuracy of 54.8% for OPT 175B with the separate-calibrated method, so the values for separate, separate-calibrated and joint are 79.1%, 54.8% and 30.2% respectively. That page does not state explicitly how many examples the 54.8% corresponds to, so it is not treated here as an ablation confirmed to share the same shot condition. The authors observe that on HellaSwag the accuracy ordering of the methods is the same for all six models tested, but in other multiple-choice scenarios different models may prefer different adaptations. [Figure 33 and §8.2, PDF pp. 58–59](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=58)

The **specific content** of the examples also affects results. In the open-book NaturalQuestions scenario, the paper gives the model relevant material and asks it to answer questions. For davinci 175B, three different draws of in-context examples give F1 scores of 0.376, 0.611 and 0.636. These are three draws of examples for the same model and scenario, not three test sets. F1 measures the word overlap between the answer and the reference answer, which means something different from the proportion of multiple-choice items answered correctly. [Figure 31 and §8.2, PDF p. 56](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=56)

When examples are needed, the paper usually includes at most five; if the context window cannot hold them, fewer are actually used. The authors also vary the number of examples and the prompt format for some models and scenarios. The analysis is constrained by cost and does not exhaust every prompt for every model. The lesson is that a score belongs both to the model and to the specific evaluation protocol that produced it. [§7, §8.2, PDF pp. 46, 56–57](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=46)

In Figure 33 of the paper, the same kinds of adaptation are also compared across several multiple-choice scenarios. HellaSwag items naturally resemble continuing an unfinished text, and the authors consider separate scoring well suited to this form; this is an interpretation of the observed results, not an additional causal test. In scenarios such as OpenBookQA, the Anthropic model relatively prefers the joint method, while most of the other five models tested prefer the separate-calibrated method. Adaptation rules, scenarios and models thus appear to interact: the same rule changes different models by different amounts. [§8.2, PDF pp. 58–59](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=58)

This poses a practical trade-off for comparison design. Choosing the same path for every model allows direct comparison of behavior under the same input rules; picking each model's own best path is closer to asking "what is the best it can do among the specified options", but then the comparison also includes different amounts of adaptation work. Keeping these two questions apart in a report is clearer than permanently declaring one of them the only fair method.

The NQ example also shows that reporting the variation, beyond the mean, is meaningful. Looking at only one of the three draws, a reader might attribute a change caused by the choice of examples to a stable difference in model ability. In each run the authors use the same examples for every test instance, then repeat the run with different random selections; this makes it possible to estimate how much the choice of example set affects the result. Three runs provide one window of observation, and the maximum and minimum shown cover only these three sets of examples.

The number of examples and the content of examples are two different variables. Adding examples may help the model understand the output format, while choosing different examples may change the task cues it sees. The paper's experiment on example counts allows at most 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 examples, constrained by context length; the trends it observes vary by task and cannot be reduced to "more examples is always better". [Figure 32 and §8.2, PDF p. 57](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=57) The next question is: even with the protocol fixed, what exactly do we use to judge whether a result is good?

## From one score to seven kinds of metric

HELM holds that a practical system needs to meet many requirements, of which answering correctly is only one. The authors compute several metrics in the same scenario wherever possible, so that one can see whether different requirements support each other or trade off. The paper organizes the selected measurements into seven categories: [§4, PDF pp. 25–27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=25)

| Metric category | What it asks | Specific examples in the paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | How close is the model's output to what the task requires? | Proportion of multiple-choice items correct, QA F1, retrieval ranking, summarization ROUGE |
| Calibration | Do the probabilities assigned to predictions match how often they are actually correct? | Expected calibration error comparing probability with accuracy |
| Robustness | How does task performance hold up after specified changes to the input? | Spelling or phrasing perturbations, and contrast sets that change the correct answer |
| Fairness | How does performance fare under group-related changes or on group subsets? | Counterfactual substitution, comparing performance by existing group metadata |
| Social bias | Is the representation and association of groups in generated text unbalanced? | Frequency of group-related words and their co-occurrence with occupation words |
| Toxicity | Is the generated content detected as toxic? | Detecting generated text with the Perspective API |
| Efficiency | What resources are needed to obtain these results? | Inference time, estimates of training energy and emissions |

"Accuracy" is an umbrella term in the paper. For multiple-choice items one can count the proportion correct; question answering uses F1, which looks at word overlap; retrieval uses ranking metrics; and summarization uses automatic metrics such as ROUGE. All serve to evaluate task performance, but they should not be read as the same quantity. Whether a summarization score is high or low also depends on whether the reference texts and automatic metrics capture the qualities readers actually care about.

The authors chose 16 **core scenarios**, aiming for each to cover as many of the seven requirements as possible. "Core" here refers to how the scenarios are organized for joint multi-metric measurement; the other scenarios are just as important. Beyond these there are 26 additional scenarios forming seven targeted evaluations, which go deeper into abilities and risks such as knowledge, reasoning, memorization and copyright; there are 42 scenarios in total. [§1.1, PDF pp. 3–4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=3)

With the 16 scenarios as rows and the seven metric categories as columns, there are in principle 16×7 = 112 "scenario–metric category" combinations. The paper reports that 98 of them are measured, 87.5%. This does not mean every specific sub-metric in each category is present in every row; Table 4 also lists the finer sub-metrics. It also differs from the earlier 96.0%, which concerned how many scenarios each of the 30 models took part in. [Table 4, PDF p. 27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=27)

Why are 14 combinations left unmeasured? Most are undefined under the adaptation used in that scenario. For example, separate adaptation reads candidate probabilities and generates no answer, so there is no generated text from which to compute generation toxicity. For the remaining gaps, the authors worry about the validity of the measurement, for example because it is hard to ensure that automatic perturbations of long summaries still satisfy the intended conditions. These differ in nature from the model–scenario technical gaps caused by model interfaces.

The seven kinds of metric are a set of complementary questions, and aggregating them requires thinking about uses and the weight of each. Calibration in particular neither counts how many answers are correct nor checks whether the model claims in words "I am confident".

Review the matrix with the homeostasis item. Accuracy needs to know which answer the model chose and what the reference answer is; calibration additionally needs the model's probability for that prediction; robustness needs related changes produced by a method defined in advance, and then a check of task performance before and after. Each additional requirement adds a measurement procedure; it does not just rename the same "correct" record.

Generation tasks bring another kind of difficulty. Whether a summary is good involves whether it keeps the important information of the source, whether it introduces unsupported content and whether its expression is clear. The paper uses automatic metrics to evaluate dimensions such as quality and faithfulness, but the authors also point out the limitations of automatic metrics. A computable number makes comparison possible; whether it adequately represents the quality readers care about still requires evidence. [§3.5, PDF pp. 21–22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=21)

QA F1 and multiple-choice accuracy also help explain why the "accuracy" category has several implementations. A multiple-choice system ends by choosing one candidate, which can be checked against the reference option; the output of free-form question answering may overlap partially with the reference answer, and a word-overlap metric can distinguish an answer with no overlap at all from one containing some of the reference words. It still depends on the reference answer and on surface word processing, and no single number can cover every reasonable phrasing.

Retrieval is different again: the system has to rank several candidate documents, and relevant documents ranked near the top are usually more useful. Summarization has to be compared with reference summaries or the source text, and automatic metrics capture only the properties they were designed for. Putting all of these under "accuracy" serves to find the usual task-performance metric for each task; when interpreting a QA result of 0.7 and a summarization result of 0.7, each one's definition still has to be kept in mind. [§3.4–3.5, §4.3, PDF pp. 20–22, 27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=20)

Efficiency involves factors beyond the model's parameters. Training energy and emissions require information about training hardware, duration and energy; the paper uses disclosed values where available and estimates from assumptions where not. Inference time is also affected by model architecture, hardware and request length. Costs along different dimensions should be read together with the actual results. [§4.9, PDF p. 33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=33)

Resource metrics also help explain why evaluation itself involves trade-offs. Querying models takes time and computation, and paid interfaces may incur charges. The authors use both open models and models with different access conditions, so expanding scenarios, examples and prompt variants all require budget. The more comprehensive the measurement, the more it needs to be explicit about which combinations were run and which gaps remain in the results, so that readers can see the actual coverage. [§1.2, §6, PDF pp. 6, 45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=6) The next section works through calibration by hand with Figure 17, to see how one kind of metric is obtained from model outputs.

## Figure 17: What calibration and selective classification each compute

![Figure 17 shows eight predictions with probabilities and correctness: 0.0 correct, 0.1 incorrect, 0.2 incorrect, 0.3 correct, 0.7 correct, 0.8 incorrect, 0.9 correct and 1.0 correct. Two equal groups have accuracy 0.5 and mean probability 0.15, error 0.35, and accuracy 0.75 and mean probability 0.85, error 0.10. Their weighted ECE is 0.225. The lower panel selects 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0; two are correct, giving selective accuracy 2/3.](assets/helm/figure-17-calibration.webp "Original Figure 17: Calculating calibration error and selective classification")

Calibration asks: can the probability a model assigns to its own predictions reflect how often it is correct in the long run? If a batch of predictions all carry a probability of 0.8 and about eight in ten are correct, that matches what the probability means; if only half are correct, the model is overconfident on this batch. Accuracy tells us how many answers are correct; calibration also considers whether the model's confidence in its predictions matches the outcomes. [§4.4, PDF pp. 27–28](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=27)

The upper half of Figure 17 lists eight predictions. Each number is the probability the model assigns to the answer it chose, and a green tick or red cross shows whether that prediction was correct. The authors divide them into two equal-sized groups of four by probability: the first group is 0.0, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3, and the second is 0.7, 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0. These are example values given in the original figure for explanation, not experimental results from some model.

Compute the first group. Two of the four predictions are correct, so accuracy is 2/4 = 0.5; the mean probability is (0.0 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.3)/4 = 0.15. In this group the model gives a probability of 0.15 on average, while the actual accuracy is 0.5, an absolute difference of 0.35. It reflects how far this group's mean probability is from the observed frequency of being correct.

The second group has three correct, so accuracy is 3/4 = 0.75; the mean probability is (0.7 + 0.8 + 0.9 + 1.0)/4 = 0.85. The absolute difference is 0.10. The first group's mean probability is below its actual accuracy, and the second's is above; taking absolute values, we measure the size of the deviation, so positive and negative directions do not cancel out.

Expected calibration error (ECE) weights each group's error by the share of all samples that fall in that group. Each group has four samples, so the ECE for this figure is:

$$
\frac{4}{8}\times0.35+\frac{4}{8}\times0.10=0.225
$$

This value describes the calibration error of the illustrated samples under this grouping, and lower values mean the groups' mean probabilities are closer to their frequencies of being correct. The figure uses only two groups to make hand calculation easy; the paper's default is ten groups, each containing an equal number of probabilities, rather than ten equal-width intervals cut from 0 to 1. [Figure 17 and §4.4, PDF p. 28](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=28)

The lower half of the figure turns to a different question: if the system handles only the portion of samples the model is most confident about and passes the rest to people, how accurate is that portion? The figure circles the three highest probabilities, 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0, corresponding to one error and two correct, so selective classification accuracy is 2/3. This selection fraction of 3/8 is just how the example is drawn; the paper separately reports accuracy at a selection fraction of C = 0.1, and the area under the coverage–accuracy curve.

The difference between the two measurements matters. A model may report probabilities that are all too high and still rank easy questions ahead of hard ones. Its numerical calibration may be poor, while the samples picked out by sorting on confidence are still fairly accurate. Conversely, knowing that the groups are well calibrated on average does not let one judge the risk of each individual item from that average alone. Calibration looks at the correspondence between probability values and frequencies; selective classification looks at what results from filtering on this signal.

If low-confidence samples go to human review and the model handles the rest, lowering coverage increases the human workload. When comparing selective classification, then, state both the fraction kept and the corresponding accuracy. The coverage–accuracy curve puts the changes at different retention fractions together, showing the relation between the quality of filtering and the volume handled.

ECE summarizes each group's probabilities and correctness, and also hides some of the variation within groups. When comparing ECE across papers, check the samples, the number of groups and the grouping method: grouping changes which predictions are put together, and the accuracy within a small group can fluctuate. Figure 17 makes it clear how this summary connects predicted probabilities with actual outcomes.

## Perturbations, group performance and generation risks

Calibration deals with the model's probabilistic judgments about its predictions; robustness asks whether performance holds when the input changes. The authors consider two different kinds of change. The first is invariance: changing spelling, capitalization, synonyms and the like, while expecting the correct answer to stay the same. The second is equivariance: using contrast examples that change the meaning and therefore require a different correct answer, and checking whether the model changes appropriately. [§4.5, PDF pp. 28–30](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=28)

For example, if an item merely contains a spelling error, the ideal answer is still the same as for the original; but if a contrast item changes a key fact, giving the original answer may be wrong. The paper looks at worst-case performance across a specified set of perturbations, so a model has to do well under all of these changes to get a high score. This is a measurement of local robustness near the examples, under the chosen changes; it does not cover every distribution shift or attack.

Fairness has two measurement paths in this paper. The first is counterfactual perturbation: substitute group-related expressions and look at task performance. The second, when the data already has group metadata, is to report performance on each subset separately. The two have different limitations: substitution allows a controlled comparison of changes in wording, but real group data may have more complex distributional differences; the metadata method depends on which labels a dataset actually provides. [§4.6, PDF pp. 30–31](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=30)

The authors separate fairness from generation bias. Fairness here concerns differences in task performance; generation bias concerns whether group words appear in a balanced way in the model's generated text, and whether group words form unbalanced associations with words such as occupations. Word lists and co-occurrence counts are easily affected by phrasing, so they offer only one operational perspective and cannot exhaust social bias. Toxicity is measured by running the Perspective API on generated text; the detector has known limitations of its own, and its results may differ from human judgments. [§4.7–4.8, PDF pp. 31–33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=31)

These categories have to be kept apart because a model may have similar group accuracies on a task and still generate content with stereotyped associations; or it may have low toxicity under one detector and show another kind of risk. In evaluation, the data, perturbations and detection methods behind a metric's name matter as much as the number itself.

Note also the relation between evaluation scenarios and measurement methods. Classification tasks usually have clear labels, so after a perturbation one can compare whether a label still applies; long-form generation involves many possibly suitable outputs, so judging how a given change should affect a whole answer is harder. This is exactly the validity concern the authors raise when they do not compute certain perturbation metrics for summarization. When a result cell is missing, first ask whether the method applies; a blank cell means the item was not reported, which differs from a score of zero. [Table 4, PDF p. 27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=27)

## How different metrics relate in the results

With many metrics, the authors can ask: are more accurate models usually better on other dimensions too? Figure 24 of the paper puts accuracy on the horizontal axis and, in six small plots, puts calibration error, robustness, fairness, gender representation bias, toxicity and denoised inference time on the vertical axes, with different colors for different scenarios. Each kind of point has to be understood back in its scenario and under its metric's definition. [Figure 24 and §8.1, PDF p. 48](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=48)

When reading the figure, look at direction first. For accuracy and the robustness and fairness performance scores shown here, higher is better; for calibration error, gender representation bias, toxicity and inference time, lower is better. A rise on the vertical axis therefore sometimes means improvement and sometimes means more error or cost. In particular, the fairness performance score and the generation bias score must not be treated as the same quantity.

Accuracy and calibration provide a clear contrast. Among the models tested, higher accuracy on HellaSwag tends to go with higher calibration error; OpenBookQA is another multiple-choice scenario on scientific knowledge, where the observed trend is that accuracy and calibration improve together. These relations also depend on the adaptation method. The associations in the figure compare points for different models under a particular scenario and protocol; one cannot conclude directly that training a model to be more accurate will necessarily make its calibration worse. [§1.2, §8.1, PDF pp. 6, 48–50](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=6)

The authors also report fairly strong correlations among accuracy, robustness and fairness performance, but they remind readers to note the definitions of these metrics: robustness and fairness here are both about task performance under perturbation. Even with an overall correlation, the most accurate model is not necessarily best on every dimension. Generation bias, toxicity and efficiency show differences across scenarios to varying degrees. The value of multi-metric measurement lies precisely in exposing these differences, so that readers can see the direction of each curve. [§1.2, §8.1, PDF pp. 7, 48–50](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=7)

This also explains why specific results and their original conditions need to be kept. Average trends show us overall relations; looking scenario by scenario can reveal opposite phenomena on a particular task.

For example, mixing the model points for HellaSwag and OpenBookQA into one cloud might show only a vague overall trend and hide the difference in calibration relations within the two tasks. Coloring by scenario keeps these relations distinguishable. Only when correlation analysis is then done across models within the same scenario does the question change from "what do the mixed results look like" to "how do the two metrics move together in this kind of task".

Metrics also differ in scale. F1, accuracy, toxicity proportions and seconds all have different units, and each axis's scale has to be understood on its own terms. An axis answers how that metric itself changes; explaining a trade-off also requires knowing what the change means for the application. For example, whether a fraction of a second of inference time is worth trading for a few percentage points of accuracy depends on the scenario and the conditions of use. Next, the unit of observation shifts from metrics within one scenario to comparisons between models.

## Conditions for comparing and aggregating models

The point of a unified protocol is to put models through comparable tests. HELM applies the same scenarios, metrics and the same five-example prompting rule to the selected models, giving many models that previously had no common results something to be compared against. But the models differ in training method, available data, compute budget and design goals. The authors give the example of T0++, originally designed for zero-shot prompting, which was evaluated under the five-example setting in the unified evaluation; this shows that a uniform rule and letting each model perform at its best are two different goals. [§6–7, PDF p. 45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=45)

Another result in Figure 33 shows how the choice of rules can change a comparison. On OpenBookQA, the authors compare OPT 175B with Anthropic-LM v4-s3: under the separate-calibrated method, OPT scores 58.6% and Anthropic 55.8%; under the joint method, they score 30.9% and 69.6%. Each method answers "under this interface, who does better": the former gap is 2.8 percentage points and the latter 38.7. Reporting only one of these gaps would hide the models' different sensitivity to the interface. [§8.2, PDF pp. 58–59](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=58)

Scale and cost also need to be read together. Within the same model family, the authors observe that larger models are usually more accurate but also more expensive to train and run; across families, parameter count alone is a poor predictor of downstream accuracy. Different models may also use different training objectives, data and human feedback, so differences in performance across families cannot simply be attributed to parameter count. [§1.2, §8.1, PDF pp. 7, 53–55](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=7)

Many scenarios and many metrics bring another problem: how to compress the whole results matrix into one overall score? If one model is more accurate in some scenario and another uses fewer resources, which is more suitable depends on the application's budget and risks. The authors argue that no universal aggregation method can satisfy all values, preferences and use cases; a single number can help with decisions, but the choice of its weights needs to be visible. [§11.3, PDF p. 82](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=82)

Aggregation can be thought of as two choices. The first decides which tasks, models and metrics enter the comparison: if a model lacks a required probability, its absence from the related metrics needs to be kept as an absence, not defaulted to zero. The second decides how to weigh the remaining results: a latency-sensitive use may care more about inference time, while another use may care more about misjudgments and group differences. Both choices need to be made public for readers to understand the final ranking.

The paper does not reject aggregation. The authors themselves use pairwise win rates to help browse the results, acknowledging the convenience of a single number in practical decisions. Their view is that summaries should coexist with the underlying detail: use the overview to find differences worth pursuing, then go back to the specific scenarios, models, prompts and predictions.

The same thinking applies to efficiency. Inference time can vary with input and output length, and a summarization task and a multiple-choice task usually have requests of different sizes. The paper therefore presents the relation between accuracy and denoised inference time scenario by scenario in Figure 24, reminding readers that hardware and model architecture both affect efficiency. Before comparing two points, confirming which scenario they come from means more than saying directly "this model is so many times faster". [Figure 24 and §8.1, PDF pp. 48–50](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=48) Readers can then judge afresh for their own uses, without taking any one set of weights as a universal fact. If a new scenario is added in future, the results matrix can gain a corresponding row, and one can check whether the earlier overall judgment still holds. Evaluation thus becomes a process that can be continually supplemented and revised.

The authors also use scenario-by-scenario pairwise comparisons to organize the large body of results. For a given metric and model, Figure 26 compares it with every other model, pair by pair, in each core scenario. The unit of one comparison is "one scenario, one rival model", and the proportion in the caption is the number of comparisons in which this model's metric value is higher, divided by the number of pairwise comparisons. High values of accuracy, robustness and fairness are good; calibration error, bias and toxicity have to be read the other way: a high proportion of higher values means a higher error or risk metric. Efficiency is not included in Figure 26's pairwise summary, and the authors stress that it has to be considered together with performance. [Figure 26 and §8.1, PDF pp. 50–51](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=50)

Walk through it with a teaching example made up for the guide. Considering only accuracy, model A has results against two rivals, B and C, in two scenarios, giving four comparisons. Suppose A is higher than both B and C in the first scenario, and higher than B but lower than C in the second; then it is higher three times, a proportion of 3/4. Looking at ECE instead, one first compares the two models' ECE values and then interprets them in the direction "lower error is better"; a higher error corresponds to worse, not better, calibration. If only the first scenario were included, A's proportion would be 2/2; it becomes 3/4 only when the second scenario is added. The summary proportion therefore depends on which scenarios and rivals are included, and has to be understood together with these comparison objects. This example illustrates only the unit of comparison and the denominator; it is not Figure 26's actual model data. The caption does not explain how missing results and ties are counted; a real summary requires finding out these rules, and one cannot assume that every model has the same number of valid comparisons, or treat a missing result as a score of zero.

When reading model comparisons, then, start by keeping one complete relation intact: **in which scenario, with what adaptation and by which metric, what difference appeared between the models**. This explains the results better than treating a cross-task overall score as a fixed property of a model, and it also makes it easier for others to check.

For an application that requires human review, for example, the coverage of selective classification and the accuracy of the retained samples may matter a great deal; for an application that needs large numbers of fast queries, inference time may be the main constraint. The results matrix HELM provides lets both users pick out the relevant evidence from the same evaluation, while seeing which dimensions each of them ignores. A specific deployment still needs separate validation on real data and real workflows.

## Where the evaluation's boundaries lie

HELM's scenarios are broader than those of many earlier evaluations, but they are still a subset of chosen tasks. This version mainly evaluates English, with some English varieties also considered; coverage of non-English languages and of English varieties in socially important applications remains insufficient. The authors also point out gaps in domains such as clinical medicine, finance and education, and in new kinds of generative application. Although QuAC contains questions in dialogue form, using such data does not amount to evaluating a real multi-turn interactive system. [§3.2, §10.1, PDF pp. 19, 78–79](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=19)

Train–test contamination is another hard problem. If a model saw the test content, or a closely similar distribution, during training, the score may include effects of memorization. Most models' training data are inadequately disclosed; the paper marks the known evidence, but the overall degree of contamination and its effect on the results remain unclear. This unknown requires readers interpreting the unified tests to bear in mind that each model's prior exposure to data may differ. [§6, §11.1, PDF pp. 45, 81](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=45)

Cost also affected the precision of the evidence. The authors used three sets of random examples to estimate the variation due to prompt selection, and limited the number of test instances in the evaluation; the paper notes that the commonly used three random seeds and 1000 instances may not be enough for every difference to reach statistical significance. The sensitivity to some details of prompting or decoding was also not fully examined. Evaluation has to consider validity, whether a metric measures what it is meant to measure, and reliability, how large the accidental variations that should not affect results are. [§7, §11.2, PDF pp. 46, 81–82](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=46)

These gaps are also part of the authors' demand for transparent evaluation: only by stating clearly what has been measured can one decide what evidence is needed next. When using the results, first find the scenarios and metrics relevant to the actual use, then look at the protocol, examples and limitations behind those results.

Validity also includes a more basic question: do the items in a dataset represent the actual task we care about? A news summarization benchmark, for instance, can test performance on news text, but it does not automatically represent summarization quality on clinical notes, teaching materials or complex long documents. Even within "summarization", input sources, lengths, reference answers and the consequences of errors can all differ. Breaking scenarios into task, domain and language is meant precisely to make such differences visible.

Data labels have limitations too. Judging toxicity involves context and the annotators' understanding of harmful expression, and without background a sentence may be hard to judge reliably. In choosing tasks, the paper discusses these problems with toxicity-detection data; when an automatic detector was later used to evaluate generated text, the detector's own definitions and errors entered the measurement process as well. "Measured toxicity" should therefore be reported together with the tool, inputs and evaluation context used. [§3.7, §4.8, PDF pp. 23–24, 32–33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09110v2#page=23)

The treatment of English varieties involves similar choices of measurement. The paper considers some English varieties in language-modeling data and automatic perturbations, but the authors hope to measure them further in actual uses. Language, application and metric intersect: covering a language and a task separately is not the same as validating their combination, which is what it takes to answer how well the language is served in that task.

Reliability reminds us to distinguish gaps between models from fluctuations in measurement. Different draws of a few examples already produced visible changes in scores on NQ; test examples, annotations and prompt formats can also affect results. More thorough repetition, significance analysis and data from applicable scenarios would make later comparisons more convincing.

The interaction gap also concerns what is being evaluated. A static dataset can contain a past conversation and ask the model to answer one question within it; a real interactive system also faces its own answers changing later states, follow-up questions from users and feedback from the external environment. A score on a static task is evidence about the given examples and protocol; judging an interactive system calls for a separately designed evaluation that matches its actions and feedback.
