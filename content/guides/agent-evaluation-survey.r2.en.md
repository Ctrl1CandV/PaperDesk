# Agent Evaluation Survey: Define the System Before Interpreting the Score

An evaluation table lists some agent's success rate. Press further and the question splits into several layers. Is the comparison between models alone, or between whole systems made of a model plus tools, prompts, memory and a retry strategy? Where do the tasks come from, does the environment actually change state during the run, and who judges success afterward? With the same model, a different tool description, a different way of simulating the user or a different retry budget changes the score. Once a language model is placed in a multi-step process, shares state and operates on an environment, the object of evaluation has changed from one answer to a sequence of actions with consequences.

Researchers from IBM Research, Yale University and the Hebrew University released this survey in March 2025; its abstract calls it the first systematic survey of evaluation methods for agents of this kind. It divides the material into four layers: fundamental capabilities (planning and multi-step reasoning, function calling and tool use, self-reflection, memory), four kinds of application (web, software engineering, science, conversational services), generalist agent benchmarks, and the evaluation frameworks used during development; a final section discusses trends and gaps. [Abstract and §1, PDF p. 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=1)

The survey is worth reading now because what it offers is coordinates, not a leaderboard. Single-turn text benchmarks such as MMLU and GSM8K are outside its scope; the authors concentrate on problems that appear only "once a model is put inside a system". After reading it, when facing a new agent benchmark paper, you can first ask a few concrete questions: what does it define the task, the environment and the judgment to be; does its criterion of success fall on the final result, on individual steps or on the whole trajectory; and were the two systems being compared run under the same conditions? The answers to these questions usually decide how useful the result is to you more than the column of numbers on the leaderboard.

The main line of the survey is "define the system under test first, then state the granularity of the evidence", and the guide follows that line. It first looks at the layered structure of Figure 1, then uses a section to explain the three things one measurement consists of; next it follows the survey's own chapter order through capabilities, applications, generalist agents and evaluation frameworks; then it compares the three granularities of "outcome, step and trajectory" together, and finally returns to the gaps the authors list and a quick-reference table. To give abstract definitions something to hold on to, the guide prepares an invented teaching example: the customer-service agent of a fictional airline named "Blue Falcon Airlines". The booking numbers, policy clauses and values are all made up and were not run as an experiment. Abbreviations are underlined with a dotted line on first use in the text; clicking one shows its full name and origin.

This guide is pinned to the [full text of arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1) of 20 March 2025. The survey was later published in Findings of ACL 2026, and according to the publication record the formal version is organized around five perspectives; this guide uses only v1's four organizing directions and its discussion section, and does not mix the classifications, sections and page numbers of the two versions. [v1 version record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16416v1) · [Findings of ACL 2026 publication record](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1330/)

Figure 1 is a tree that grows from left to right. At the far left is the root node, Agent Evaluation, which splits into five main branches to the right, each labeled with its section number: Agent Capabilities Evaluation (§2), Application-Specific Agent Evaluation (§3), Generalist Agents Evaluation (§4), Frameworks for Agent Evaluation (§5) and Discussion (§6). The blue boxes on the branches are subtopics, and the cells at the far right are densely packed with cited papers and benchmark names. [Figure 1 and §1, PDF p. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=2)

There is an economical order for reading this figure: read the boxes first, then the cells on the right. The boxes tell you into which layers of questions the survey cuts "evaluation"; the cells on the right only list which works exist under each layer and provide no conclusions themselves. The real judgments are in the text, for example why a given benchmark measures only a capability and not task completion.

**The capability layer (§2)** is divided into four branches by the fundamental capabilities agents depend on: planning and multi-step reasoning, function calling and tool use, self-reflection, and memory. Most names in the right-hand cells are the first benchmarks you will meet when reading papers: GSM8K, HotpotQA and PlanBench under planning; BFCL, ToolBench and ToolSandbox under tools; LLF-Bench and Reflection-Bench under reflection; ReadAgent, MemGPT and LoCoMo under memory. This layer measures mechanisms: whether a given capability works under controlled conditions.

**The application layer (§3)** is divided by deployment domain into four branches: web, software engineering, scientific tasks and conversational services. Benchmarks at this layer usually have their own environments, tool interfaces and judging rules: web benchmarks look at the state of a site, software-engineering benchmarks at repositories and tests, and conversational-service benchmarks at databases and domain policies. The same "success rate" means four different things here.

**The generalist layer (§4)** puts many tasks or environments together to see whether a system can transfer to conditions it has not seen; representative works include GAIA, AgentBench, OSWorld and TheAgentCompany.

**The framework layer (§5)** serves the development process and splits into two branches, development frameworks and Gym-like environments. Its difference from the first three layers is that a benchmark gives you fixed items, while a framework lets you evaluate scenarios you wrote yourself and embeds evaluation into debugging, release and monitoring.

**The discussion layer (§6)** draws things together in two sections: current trends (more realistic, harder evaluations, and continually updated live benchmarks) and emerging directions (fine-grained evaluation, cost-efficiency, scaling and automation, safety and compliance).

These five branches answer "at which layer evaluation sits", not "which benchmark is best". The four layers share one thread, which is also the question the survey keeps coming back to: a measurement must be able to state what the system under test includes, how the task, environment and metrics are each defined, and at what granularity the evidence falls. The next section deals with the first two.

## First define the system under test and the three components

Before discussing specific benchmarks, two things that are often mixed up need to be separated. A large model provides generation and reasoning; an agent built around it chains many model calls into one process, keeps shared state between calls so that context stays consistent, and uses external tools to compute, fetch knowledge and change the environment. In the introduction the authors use this distinction to define the survey's scope: an agent's abilities come from a specific large model, but it acts sequentially in a dynamic environment, and therefore needs new evaluation methods, benchmarks, environments and metrics. [§1, PDF p. 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=1)

For a specific measurement, this means that reporting a score requires at least stating the system configuration: model version, tool list and descriptions, policy or system prompt, memory mechanism, and retry and call budget. Without these, readers cannot judge whether a change happened in the model, in the tool descriptions or in limits on the process. The fictional Blue Falcon customer-service system illustrates the difference. For the same booking, the model might explain the change rules correctly but pick the wrong booking; it might pick the right booking but write a flight number into the date argument; or it might complete the change but fail to tell the user the new gate. All three end in a poor experience, yet the directions for improvement are completely different.

Next come the three components the survey gives at the start of its application chapter. The authors argue that agent benchmarks can evaluate capabilities systematically because they provide three things at once. A set of well-defined tasks says what the agent is to achieve. An environment, which may be static or dynamic, simulated or real, may contain user simulation, a set of tools and policies to follow. And evaluation metrics, such as success rate, efficiency and accuracy, can be applied at different granularities, from individual actions and milestones all the way to end-to-end task completion. [§3, PDF p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=6)

Filling the Blue Falcon example into these three slots makes it easier to see what each one constrains:

| Component | Content in this example | What it constrains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task | Move booking LB-2043 from tomorrow's 15:00 flight to the same time the day after, and choose a window seat | What the goal is, what counts as done |
| Environment | Booking and flight databases, change and seat-selection interfaces, a policy written in the system prompt, a simulated user. The policy: Lite fares cannot be changed, but a flight delayed more than 2 hours may be changed once free of charge; before a change, the name and the last four digits of the ID must be verified; after a change, the new gate and baggage allowance must be stated | What can be observed, what can be operated, which rules must be followed |
| Metrics | The date, seat and fee of the final booking, plus the new gate and baggage allowance that must be output | What judgment is used, and at what granularity it falls |

With any slot missing, the same number takes on a different meaning. If the environment compares only the conversation text and never actually writes to the booking database, "success" only means "sounded like success". If the metric checks only the date, an agent that miscalculates the fee still scores. If the task description never said "window", a failed seat change cannot be blamed on the system. This is also why, for each application domain, the survey first explains how that domain's benchmarks construct their environments and what they use to judge.

The three components also explain why the same model can rank very differently on different leaderboards. Whether the environment really writes data, whether the user is played by a person or a model, whether the policy is written in the system prompt or not provided at all, all change the difficulty of the task and the kinds of failure; choosing success rate or itemized accuracy as the metric changes "which errors cost more". Only when these conditions are written into the report can a number be reproduced by another lab. In the fictional example, if someone removed the "delay over 2 hours" policy from the environment, the same agent's behavior would change from "should make the change" to "must refuse", the success rate would change with it, and not a word of the model under test would have changed.

The measuring apparatus also has to be kept apart from the running conditions. The three components describe the apparatus; the same apparatus with different running conditions (model version, prompt and policy text, tool descriptions, number of retries, concurrency and timeouts) still yields two sets of observations, each valid on its own terms. Only when the apparatus and the running conditions are both written into the report can readers judge where a difference in scores comes from.

This framework applies to the capability layer as well, only with narrower slots: a GSM8K item also has a task (solve it), an environment (the problem text and the tools allowed) and a metric (whether the answer is correct), except that nothing in the environment changes state and the metric falls on the final answer. The framework's use is to help you see clearly what is "narrow" and what is "wide", not to divide the two kinds of benchmark into unrelated things.

## Capability evaluation: Shrinking a big question

End-to-end task failures are hard to locate: the plan may have been wrong, a tool description may have been misread, or outdated information may have been retrieved from memory. Section 2 of the survey measures these capabilities separately, pairing each kind with a set of dedicated benchmarks. This section walks through its four branches, focusing on what conditions each way of measuring fixes and what it therefore cannot see.

**Planning and multi-step reasoning** is the capability the authors put first. Multi-step reasoning usually refers to problems that need 3 to 10 intermediate steps to reach an answer and cannot be solved by a single inference. Existing benchmarks cover mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH, AQUA-RAT), multi-hop question answering (HotpotQA, StrategyQA, MultiRC), scientific reasoning (ARC), logical reasoning (FOLIO, P-FOLIO), constraint-satisfaction puzzles (Game of 24) and common sense (MUSR). Among them, HotpotQA, ALFWorld and Game of 24 have been specially adapted to evaluate approaches such as ReAct that interleave planning with tool calls. [§2.1, PDF p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=3)

More specialized planning benchmarks tighten the conditions. ToolEmu uses an emulator to evaluate tool-using agents, and the authors report its finding that successful plans require tracking state explicitly and being able to recover from errors. MINT evaluates planning in interactive environments and concludes that advanced models still struggle with long-horizon tasks that need many steps. PlanBench covers several domains and reports that models are good at short-term tactical plans but decline when long-range strategy is needed. AutoPlanBench turns to everyday scenarios and finds that the best agents of the time still lagged behind classical symbolic planners. FlowBench evaluates workflow planning, ACPBench evaluates core reasoning skills, and Natural Plan describes real-world planning tasks in natural language, with model performance getting markedly worse as complexity rises. [§2.1, PDF p. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=4)

From these results the authors distill five capabilities essential to planning: breaking complex problems into subtasks, tracking state and maintaining beliefs, detecting and correcting errors, causal understanding to predict the consequences of actions, and meta-planning to improve planning strategies. The list is the survey's synthesis and can serve as a checklist when reading a new benchmark: when a benchmark claims to test "planning", which of these does it actually press on?

**Function calling and tool use** is broken into five subtasks: recognizing when a function is needed, choosing the right tool, mapping parameter values from the conversation onto function arguments, executing the function, and writing the returned result into the reply. Early benchmarks (ToolAlpaca, APIBench, ToolBench, the first version of BFCL) used synthetic data and rule-based matching (for example comparing abstract syntax trees) to compute pass rates and structural correctness, mainly covering single-step interactions with explicitly stated arguments. They miss multi-step conversations in real settings, arguments that are never stated in the conversation but must be inferred, and tools with complex input structures and long outputs. [§2.2, PDF p. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=4)

Later versions of BFCL push this line toward real complexity: the second version adds tools from within organizations, and the third adds evaluation logic for multi-turn, multi-step interaction, shifting the focus to continuous state management. ToolSandbox goes in another direction. It makes tool execution stateful, with implicit state dependencies among tools, uses a built-in user simulator for on-policy conversational evaluation, and checks intermediate and final milestones dynamically along arbitrary trajectories. The problem it addresses is "the result of an earlier call decides whether a later one can be made", which is a different matter from measuring whether arguments are filled in correctly once. ComplexFuncBench targets scenarios that require inferring implicit arguments, following user constraints and handling long contexts; NESTFUL makes the output of one call the input of the next, testing nested call sequences specifically. [§2.2, PDF pp. 4–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=5)

Along this line, the differences among tool benchmarks are concentrated in three places: whether interaction is single-step or multi-step, whether state changes during execution, and whether arguments are stated or must be inferred. When comparing two "tool-calling accuracies", first confirm which cell each falls into. [ToolSandbox abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04682)

**Self-reflection** measures whether an agent can understand feedback, update its judgments and adjust its actions over long trajectories. Early approaches were rather indirect: reasoning or planning tasks such as AGIEval, MedMCQA, ALFWorld and MiniWoB++ were turned into multi-turn feedback loops to see whether a model could find and correct its own errors in a restricted setting. This way of measuring checks only whether the final answer was corrected, a very coarse granularity; the authors also note that observed improvements may depend on particular prompting tricks, with no common standard. [§2.3, PDF p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=5)

LLF-Bench is a standardized benchmark proposed to fill this gap. It extends a variety of decision-making tasks into interactive form and places the task instructions in the environment rather than on the agent's side; to reduce overfitting to particular environments, it can randomize the wording of task instructions and feedback. The question it answers is whether an agent can learn while acting when feedback is given in natural language and the task instructions may also vary. [LLF-Bench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06853) LLM-Evolve takes another angle, collecting past queries with feedback as in-context examples and evaluating reflection on standard items such as MMLU. Pan and colleagues focus on programming agents, extending APPS and LiveCodeBench to an interactive setting. Reflection-Bench borrows a decomposition from cognitive science, splitting reflection into components such as perceiving new information, using memory, updating beliefs after surprise, adjusting decisions, counterfactual reasoning and meta-reflection. [§2.3, PDF p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=5)

Reflection evaluations have an unavoidable condition: where the feedback comes from, what the agent can see at run time, and how many attempts are allowed. A scheme that sees more feedback and runs more rounds than its baseline owes its gain partly to "more chances to correct errors" and partly to "more computation". In discussing this kind of work, the authors treat feedback and budget as experimental conditions, and when reading results they need to be stated in the same place.

**Memory** deals with long contexts and keeping information across sessions. The authors divide memory into short-term and long-term: short-term memory supports real-time responses, and long-term memory supports understanding and use over longer spans of time. There are two lines of evaluation. One targets context length: ReadAgent groups content, compresses passages into memories and retrieves them on demand, evaluated on QUALITY, NarrativeQA and QMSum; MemGPT manages tiered memory and is tested on NaturalQuestions-Open and multi-session chat data; A-MEM's memory structure is evaluated with LoCoMo. The other targets continual improvement: StreamBench looks at whether an agent can use external memory (including earlier interactions and external feedback) to improve steadily over time, covering text-to-SQL, ToolBench, HotpotQA and other tasks; the LTM benchmark interleaves several tasks within a single long conversation to test long-term memory and the integration of information. [§2.4, PDF pp. 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=5)

The conclusions of memory evaluations have to be read with their conditions. The LTM benchmark reports that models do well on single tasks but noticeably worse once tasks are interleaved; it also reports that short-context models equipped with long-term memory systems can match or even exceed models with larger context windows. The latter result compares "memory system + small window" with "large window", which differ in call cost, retrieval overhead and the information available, so it is suited to discussing design trade-offs rather than ranking kinds of model. [LTM benchmark abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20222)

The value of the capability layer is that it fixes conditions more clearly, making "which step went wrong" observable. Its scores are evidence about mechanisms: how far a capability works under the current setup. Adding up several capability scores does not let one conclude that an end-to-end task will succeed, because real tasks also require these capabilities to connect within one trajectory, under environmental feedback, tool limits and budget constraints. To judge whether a system can complete tasks, one still has to look at measurements at the application or generalist layer, or construct a complete trajectory oneself.

## Real environments, part one: Web and software engineering

Benchmarks at the application layer put tasks back into specific environments. The survey chooses four domains; this section looks first at web and software engineering, whose common feature is that "results" can be checked by the state of the environment or by tests, and the next section looks at scientific tasks and conversational services.

**Web agents** have to operate websites to complete tasks such as booking tickets and shopping. The evolution of their evaluation can be read as environments moving from static to dynamic. The earliest, MiniWob and MiniWoB++, provide basic simulated environments for testing navigation and task automation. Then came static datasets reproducible offline: WebShop simulates a shopping flow from search all the way to checkout, and Mind2Web and WebVoyager extend to more kinds of web interaction for comparing methods. Later still came a turn to dynamic online environments: WebLinX requires agents to adapt to continually changing interfaces; WebArena and its visual version VisualWebArena set up real, usable websites and require agents to read visual information and act; WorkArena and WorkArena++ simulate multi-step tasks in an office; MMInA offers holistic multimodal, multi-hop evaluation; and AssistantBench targets time-consuming tasks across sites. [§3.1, PDF pp. 6–7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=6)

Refinement of judgment is another line. WebCanvas moves evaluation to the completion rate of key navigation nodes, which points out where an agent gets stuck better than "is the final page correct"; ST-WebAgentBench combines static and dynamic elements to observe how agents perform under various conditions. Here the authors name an explicit gap: most web benchmarks still center on task completion and navigation efficiency, with little measurement of policy compliance, risk mitigation and organizational safety protocols; yet the closer web agents get to real deployment, the more these matter. [§3.1, PDF p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=7)

The Blue Falcon example makes this gap tangible. If a web benchmark checks only "was the booking moved to the day after tomorrow", an agent that skips verifying the ID, or cancels and rebooks and charges 120 yuan more, may still count as successful. To bring such behavior into evaluation, the environment needs to be able to record the sequence of actions, and the metrics need to include policy checks.

**Software-engineering agents** start from short algorithmic problems, such as HumanEval and MBPP: given a function description, generate code that passes unit tests. Open-domain programming benchmarks later added application scenarios and multi-library dependencies, but the evaluated intents remained fairly simple and tool use limited. What really pushed tasks into real engineering was SWE-bench. It builds items from real GitHub issues and the corresponding merged pull requests, provides the problem description, the complete code repository, an execution environment (for example Docker) and validation tests, and requires the model to modify the codebase to resolve the issue. The original paper gives 2,294 problems from 12 Python repositories; resolving one often requires understanding and coordinating several functions, classes or even files. [§3.2, PDF p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=7) · [SWE-bench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770)

After SWE-bench came a whole family, each version fixing one weakness of the previous. Lite takes 300 bug-fix problems, filtering out items that need complex multi-file changes or contain extraneous elements; LiteS further removes items that already have exact patches or lack sufficient description; Verified keeps only problems with clear descriptions and reliable tests; SWE-bench+ targets two evaluation flaws, answer leakage and weak tests; and there are also a Java version and a Multimodal version for JavaScript applications with visual elements. SWT-Bench and TDD-Bench Verified change the task: the agent generates tests from an issue. ITBench evaluates IT automation tasks, and SWELancer ties freelance-style programming tasks to actual sums of money, examining long-horizon reasoning and decision-making. [§3.2, PDF p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=7)

When reading scores from this family, look at three things first: which version was used, whether the judgment is "tests pass", and how strong the tests themselves are. Passing tests is an executable, reproducible criterion, which makes SWE-bench far sturdier than "does the answer look right"; at the same time it defines success as "make this set of tests pass", so side effects the tests do not cover, maintainability and understanding of requirements are all outside the score. The existence of SWE-bench+ shows that leakage and weak tests are problems that actually happened, so when meeting a high score on an older version, it is worth confirming this first.

## Real environments, part two: Scientific tasks and conversational services

**Scientific agents** are evaluated on everything from knowledge questions to research workflows. Early benchmarks test recall of and reasoning with scientific knowledge (ARC, ScienceQA, ScienceWorld); others test the synthesis and contextualization of literature (QASPER, QASA, MS2); SciRiff extends to following user instructions across domains. Newer benchmarks turn the lens on each stage of research: proposing research ideas (are they novel, relevant, feasible?), designing experiments (AAAR-1.0 evaluates hypotheses, choice of methods and experimental procedures), writing executable code for experiments (SciCode, ScienceAgentBench, SUPER, CORE-Bench), and generating peer-review comments. [§3.3, PDF p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=8)

The approaches of two representative benchmarks deserve a closer look. ScienceAgentBench extracts 102 tasks from 44 peer-reviewed papers, covering four disciplines, and has them validated by nine domain experts; it unifies each task's output as a self-contained artifact so that it can be judged item by item. The authors of this benchmark argue that before claiming end-to-end automation of research, individual stages need to be evaluated rigorously first. [ScienceAgentBench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05080) CORE-Bench picks another concrete matter: computational reproducibility. It contains 270 tasks based on 90 papers and requires the agent to reproduce results with the code and data provided. [CORE-Bench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11363)

Another kind of work packs several stages into one platform: AAAR-1.0 puts equation inference, experiment design, identifying weaknesses in papers and writing review comments together; MLGym provides a Gym-like environment covering 13 research challenges, from hypothesis generation to analysis of experiments; DiscoveryWorld uses a virtual text environment to simulate the complete cycle of scientific discovery, with 120 tasks; and LAB-Bench targets biology, from experimental design to interpreting text, images and tables. [§3.3, PDF p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=8)

Scientific evaluations often rest on executable artifacts, which makes judgment easier to reproduce, but "the program runs" and "the conclusion holds scientifically" are two different things. CORE-Bench reports whether published results can be reproduced, and ScienceAgentBench reports task completion for individual stages; neither judges, on the authors' behalf, whether a new finding is reliable. When reading such results, first confirm which step the metric covers.

**Conversational-service agents** have to handle user requests over many turns, follow company policies and complete a series of function calls. The survey distinguishes two ways of constructing such benchmarks. The first collects real trajectories with user messages, agent messages and function calls, gives the first part, and has the agent predict the next step. The second goes further, simulating both the environment and the user, and checks whether the agent can bring the environment to the desired state and tell the user the correct answer. The first comes closer to measuring "what a human would do", and the second closer to "was the task accomplished". [§3.4, PDF p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=8)

The representative of the first construction is ABCD: more than ten thousand customer-service conversations covering 55 user intents, each corresponding to an action sequence prescribed by policy; MultiWOZ and SMCalFlow are crowdsourced task-oriented dialogue data of the same kind. In the second construction, ALMITA generates tests with an automated pipeline: at each step a large model generates intents, procedures, tool interfaces, flowcharts and conversation graphs, and prefixes are extracted from conversation paths as tests; the manually filtered version contains 14 intents and 192 conversations, 1,420 tests in all. τ-bench is constructed by hand (with a little help from models): two domains, airline and retail, each with its own database, interfaces and domain policy written in the system prompt; each task contains instructions for a simulated user, together with the expected database writes and the outputs needed for the user's questions. The dataset has 115 retail tasks and 50 airline tasks. [§3.4, PDF p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=9)

τ-bench's way of judging deserves a separate look. It compares the database state at the end of the conversation with the annotated goal state, and checks the necessary outputs. The original paper also proposes pass^k, which measures reliability by whether the same task succeeds every time over k repetitions; it reports that the advanced function-calling agents of the time succeeded on fewer than half the tasks, with pass^8 below 25% in the retail domain. [τ-bench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) IntellAgent automates this route: given a database schema and a company policy document, it builds a policy graph, samples events, simulates a conversation between the agent under test and a user agent, and finally has a critic agent analyze the conversation and give feedback. [§3.4, PDF p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=9)

The Blue Falcon example falls exactly in this cell. The database state can answer "was the booking moved to the day after tomorrow, and is the fee right", the necessary outputs can answer "were the new gate and baggage allowance given to the user", while "were the last four ID digits verified before the change" is written only in the action sequence. Using a model to simulate the user brings one more condition: the simulated user's wording and patience affect the course of the conversation, so results fluctuate when the same task is run repeatedly, which is why repeated-run metrics have to be reported separately.

## Generalist agents: What a cross-task score can show

Generalist agents combine skills such as web navigation, information retrieval and code execution to complete tasks not restricted to one domain. Evaluating such systems requires putting tasks from different sources together and observing how abilities are distributed, beyond producing a single total. Section 4 of the survey introduces representative work at this layer. [§4, PDF p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=9)

GAIA is often cited. It contains 466 realistic questions written by people, testing reasoning, multimodal understanding, web navigation and general tool use. The original paper stresses that these questions are conceptually simple for people and very hard for the AI of the time: human respondents answered 92% correctly, GPT-4 with plugins 15%, and the answers to 300 questions were held back for a leaderboard. [GAIA abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983) This design shows that it measures "whether several abilities can be chained to solve one concrete problem", which no single capability score can predict.

AgentBench puts language models into eight interactive environments (operating system, database, knowledge graph, card game, lateral-thinking puzzles, household tasks, shopping and web browsing) and observes sustained action through a unified process of observation, action and scoring. [AgentBench abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03688) The survey mentions it as a framework for interactive ability in the software-engineering section, and lists it again at the generalist layer as a multi-environment collection; its eight environments are themselves a definition of "cross-environment", and its specific protocol and metrics can be found in the library's [AgentBench guide](/read/agentbench/2#environments).

Going further toward real systems, OSWorld, OmniACT and AppWorld put tasks into complete computer-operating environments, requiring agents to coordinate actions across applications, modify code and handle control flow without causing unintended system changes. TheAgentCompany sets up an extensible small software company in which agents browse internal websites, write code, run programs and communicate with colleagues. CRMArena simulates a large customer-relationship-management environment in which data are linked across accounts, orders, knowledge articles and tickets, examining whether agents can use both interfaces and APIs to complete multi-step operations, follow domain policies and integrate information. HAL tries to aggregate several benchmarks onto one platform, covering programming, interactive applications and safety evaluation. [§4, PDF p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=9)

When aggregating across tasks, the weights decide what the total score says. Averaging directly over tasks gives domains with more items more weight; averaging within domains first and then combining expresses the choice that "every domain matters equally". Both are usable, but the purpose should be stated in the report. More importantly, a total score mixes together different environments' definitions of success: for web tasks "success" means the site reaches the goal state, for programming tasks that tests pass, and for scientific tasks that an artifact executes. A total score is suited to seeing the distribution and transfer of abilities; judging whether a particular system can be used in a particular setting still requires going back to the subscores and conditions of the corresponding domain.

Multi-agent systems add another layer of questions: is the object under test the whole team, or one role within it? A team's final success does not prove that every message was useful, nor does it tell whether the gain came from collaboration or from more model calls. In its statement of scope, the survey excludes multi-agent, embodied and game settings from its main line, mentioning them only in individual sections; the connection here is an editorial addition, and readers can see the library's [multi-agent communication survey guide](/read/multi-agent-communication-survey/2).

## Evaluation frameworks: Putting evaluation into the development process

Benchmarks compare how finished systems perform in preset scenarios; evaluation frameworks serve the development process. They provide no fixed test data; instead they let developers evaluate scenarios they wrote themselves, monitor continuously during development and deployment, and analyze errors. Section 5 of the survey divides such tools into two kinds, development frameworks and Gym-like environments, and explains how the work is divided between them. [§5, PDF p. 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=10)

Development frameworks (LangSmith, Langfuse, Google Vertex AI's evaluation service, Arize AI, Galileo Agentic Evaluation, Patronus AI, LangChain's AgentEvals, Databricks Mosaic AI and others) usually all provide trajectory monitoring, recording task completion rate, latency and execution speed, and some also throughput and memory; some tools record call chains based on OpenTelemetry, an observability standard. On top of this, they add evaluation at three levels:

- **The final reply**: a large-model judge scores the reply against preset criteria; some platforms provide proprietary judge models and also allow custom metrics.
- **A single step**: evaluates a single action or model call, checking tool choice, arguments and execution output, to help locate the source of errors; Galileo's action-advancement metric goes further and asks "did this step move the goal forward", beyond success or failure.
- **The trajectory**: compares the action sequence with an expected optimal path, checking tool choice and order; AgentEvals supports judge scoring with or without a reference trajectory, and can also check, for frameworks modeled as graphs, whether nodes and transitions match expectations.

The survey is direct about the difficulty of trajectory evaluation: it depends on reference sequences, while agent workflows are themselves stochastic, and the same task often has many valid solutions, so defining and comparing an "optimal trajectory" is hard. The difficulty of step-level evaluation lies with the judge: a specialized judge suits particular tasks but may not carry over to complex workflows, while a general judge has broad coverage but no guarantee of quality. [§5, PDF pp. 10–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=10)

Frameworks also take on data work: providing annotation tools, supporting human-in-the-loop collection of feedback from production, extracting evaluation sets from production logs, and synthesizing test cases from seed data (Patronus and Mosaic provide such features). A/B comparison puts the inputs, outputs and metrics of two runs side by side, and some platforms can aggregate across many runs, but the survey considers obtaining large-scale insight at the trajectory or step level still a hard problem.

Table 1 lays out six capabilities of eight frameworks. A tick means the framework supports the capability and a cross that it does not; the authors note that some capabilities are still at an early stage.

| Framework | Step-level evaluation | Monitoring | Trajectory evaluation | Human in the loop | Synthetic data | A/B comparison |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LangSmith | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ |
| Langfuse | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ | × | ✓ |
| Google Vertex AI | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | × | × | ✓ |
| Arize AI | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Galileo Agentic | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ | × | ✓ |
| Patronus AI | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| AgentEvals | × | × | ✓ | × | × | × |
| Mosaic AI | ✓ | ✓ | × | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

Several things can be read from the table. Step-level evaluation and monitoring are almost standard, supported by seven of the eight; trajectory evaluation is offered by only three, consistent with the difficulty described above; synthetic data is also offered by only three, concentrated in platforms that provide proprietary data capabilities; AgentEvals is the only tool that does trajectories alone, without monitoring or step-level evaluation, positioned like a dedicated judge component. The table lists no prices, deployment methods or data-retention policies, which matter just as much when choosing a tool and which the survey does not cover.

Gym-like environments are the other branch of frameworks. They borrow the idea of OpenAI Gym, putting training and evaluation into controllable simulated environments, letting agents interact with dynamic environments, and allowing the same protocol to be reused across benchmarks. The survey cites BrowserGym for web agents, MLGym for AI research tasks and SWE-Gym for software-engineering agents. [§5, PDF p. 11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=11)

The features of frameworks change quickly, and Table 1 records a snapshot of v1 in early 2025. Whether a product supports a capability today depends on whether its documentation defines "support" as a built-in feature, an extensible interface, or something you have to implement yourself.

## Outcome, step and trajectory: What each granularity answers

The word "granularity" has come up repeatedly in the preceding sections. In its section on frameworks, the survey divides evaluation into three levels, the final reply, single steps and trajectories; applied to benchmarks, the corresponding three levels are the task outcome, single actions and complete trajectories. Both places are concerned with the same question: at which level does the evidence fall? This section takes it up separately, because the same system can yield completely different conclusions at the three granularities, and the Blue Falcon example can demonstrate these situations side by side.

Suppose four configurations run the same batch of tasks:

| Run | Final booking state | Necessary outputs | Action sequence | What it reads as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Date, seat and fee all correct | New gate and baggage allowance both given | Check the booking, then the flight status, verify the last four ID digits, change, choose seat, inform | Goal reached, process compliant |
| B | Booking date unchanged | None | Checked only the fare type, refused under "Lite fares cannot be changed", never checked flight status | Goal not reached: missed the delay exception |
| C | Date, seat and fee all correct | New gate and baggage allowance both given | Changed directly without verifying the last four ID digits | Goal reached, process in violation |
| D | Date correct, 120 yuan overcharged | Only the new gate given | Cancelled and rebooked without checking the change fee | Partly reached, with a new error introduced |

**Outcome evaluation** looks only at two columns, the final state and the necessary outputs: was the goal reached, and was the information that had to be given provided? It is cheap, stable and easy to reproduce, suited to comparing systems' ability to complete large numbers of tasks. It cannot see cases like C, "right result, process in violation"; for a failure like B, it leaves only a zero, without saying whether the error was a missed status check, a missed exception, or no attempt at all. On outcome scores B and C may get zero and full marks, yet in a deployment judgment C is often more dangerous than B.

**Step-level evaluation** takes each action apart: which tool was chosen at this step, what the arguments were, and whether the return value was understood correctly. It can answer "where did the error begin", for example the cancellation in D or the missing ID check in C. All steps being correct does not guarantee task completion either: with every step locally reasonable, the system may go round in a loop, or never reach the goal state. Step scores are suited to diagnosis, not to standing in for the ability to complete tasks.

**Trajectory evaluation** looks at order, dependencies, repetition and recovery among actions: was the version read before the matching clause was looked up, did the system return to the correct branch after a failure, did it spend two redundant calls to reach the right result? It comes closest to the question "how does the system work", at the cost of complex judgment. A reference trajectory is one possible correct path, while tasks often have several valid solutions: an agent might check flight status before the booking, or the other way round, and both end with a compliant change. Forcing a step-by-step match to one standard path judges valid strategies as errors; using only a broad model judge to give a total score makes it hard to provide evidence that can be located.

**Cost and repeated runs** are a fourth kind of observation. They do not replace the first three but add conditions to them. The same success rate may come from fewer calls and shorter latency, or from allowing unlimited retries; running the same task k times and succeeding every time is a different kind of reliability from succeeding at least once. τ-bench's pass^k is a metric designed for the former, bringing repeated runs into the judgment (see the [τ-bench guide](/read/tau-bench/2#passk)). When reporting results, write down the number of tasks, the number of runs, the call budget per run and the actual time taken together, so that readers can judge under what conditions the number was obtained.

Repeated runs and average success rate answer different questions, and a very small calculation shows how far apart they can be. Suppose a task's single-run success probability is 0.75 and runs are independent; then the probability that all four runs succeed is $0.75^4\approx0.32$, and the probability that at least one succeeds is $1-0.25^4\approx1.00$. A system with a 75% single-run success rate is left with just over thirty percent under the standard of "must be right every time". The values in this calculation are set by the guide for illustration, and the independence assumption often fails in real systems: the state of the same environment and the wording of the same prompt make results across runs correlated, so actual values should be computed from observed run records, or the distribution over k runs reported directly. Its use is as a reminder: there is a distance between "right on average" and "reliably usable".

These four kinds of observation also correspond to different explanations of failure. When the outcome fails, the system may never have reached the goal, or it may have reached it but failed to say so clearly. When a step fails, the tool may have been wrong, the arguments wrong, or the return value misunderstood. When a trajectory fails, the order may have been wrong, the system may have looped or failed to recover, or the reference path may have excluded another valid approach. When cost overruns, there may have been too many retries, or every call may have read an overly long context. Only by separating these explanations can one decide whether the next step is to change the prompt, the tool descriptions, the process, or the evaluation itself.

Putting these four observations together gives a table of trade-offs:

| Level of observation | Helps answer | Evidence in this example | Still needs supplementing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Final outcome | Was the goal reached, was the necessary information given? | A and C score; B and D lose points | Was the process compliant, were unobserved side effects introduced? |
| Single action | Were tools, arguments and judgments appropriate? | C lacks the ID check; D has an extra cancellation | The role of this step in the whole task |
| Complete trajectory | Order, dependencies, recovery and redundancy | A's dependency order, D's detour | Does the reference path accommodate other valid solutions? |
| Cost and repeated runs | Is it stable and affordable under the same constraints? | Number of calls, time taken, whether all k runs succeed | Task distribution, budget accounting and evaluation version |

This table was compiled by the guide from the discussion in Section 5 of the survey; it is not a table from the paper. Its use is to decide first in which cell your conclusion falls, then check whether the evidence for that cell was recorded. A conclusion falling under "final outcome" speaks about task completion; one under "trajectory" speaks about process compliance and the quality of strategy; the two can hold at the same time, or conflict.

## Cost, safety and the gaps the authors list

The survey's last section writes observed changes and unsolved problems separately. The former are trends that have already happened in benchmark design, and the latter are directions the authors think deserve investment; both are judgments from early 2025 and should be read in light of later developments. [§6, PDF p. 11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=11)

**One trend is toward more realism and more difficulty.** Early evaluations mostly used simplified, static environments; now they are moving toward settings closer to real complexity. Web benchmarks went from MiniWob to WebArena and VisualWebArena, science from the static and narrow LAB-Bench to DiscoveryWorld, software engineering replaced synthetic programming problems with real GitHub issues, and Natural Plan even introduces simulated returns from real tools such as Google Calendar and Maps. At the same time task difficulty is rising: SWE-bench, SWELancer, CORE-Bench, GAIA and TheAgentCompany all belong to this wave, and the authors illustrate the difficulty by noting that "the best-performing agents sometimes score as low as 2%".

**A second trend is live benchmarks.** Model progress quickly saturates static benchmarks, which then lose their power to discriminate, so continually updated practices have appeared: BFCL went from its first version to adding organizational tools and then multi-turn, multi-step logic; the SWE-bench family keeps spawning versions such as Lite, Verified and +; and IntellAgent builds on τ-bench to keep generating new tests. Such updates keep benchmarks relevant, and also impose a requirement on use: scores must be reported together with the version.

**One direction is finer granularity.** The authors note that many benchmarks give only end-to-end success rates, which show overall performance but cannot diagnose specific failures, so the quality of intermediate tool choices and reasoning is hidden. They recommend developing standardized fine-grained metrics that record the trajectory of task execution, like WebCanvas's node checks or the step-level evaluation in frameworks such as LangSmith and Galileo.

**A second direction is cost and efficiency.** The survey cites the observation by Kapoor and colleagues that evaluations of the time emphasized accuracy and neglected cost and efficiency, which may push systems to become stronger but more expensive and hard to deploy in practice. The authors recommend making token usage, API fees, inference time and overall resource consumption core metrics, and establishing a unified way of accounting for cost.

**A third direction is scaling and automation.** Static evaluation that depends on human annotation is expensive and slow to update. The authors propose two routes: using synthetic data to generate diverse, realistic task scenarios (IntellAgent and Mosaic already make similar attempts), and having agents act as judges (Agent-as-a-Judge), obtaining continuous, fine-grained and low-cost evaluation through automation. The latter reduces human work but may also inherit the judge's own biases; the survey raises this only as a direction and offers no validation.

**A fourth direction is safety and compliance.** The authors point out that current benchmarks pay limited attention to safety, trustworthiness and policy compliance. Some early work has begun to cover these, for example AgentHarm, which tests harmful behavior when agents are misused, and ST-WebAgentBench, which brings policy compliance into web tasks; but robustness to adversarial inputs, bias mitigation and compliance with organizational and social policies still lack systematic testing. Multi-agent settings may also give rise to new emergent risks that need dedicated design. The original AgentHarm paper lists 110 explicitly harmful agent tasks (440 with augmentation) covering 11 categories of harm, which shows that "safety evaluation" itself also needs its own methods for constructing tasks and judging them. [AgentHarm abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09024)

Put together, these show what the authors care about in common: for a score to support a specific judgment, one needs to state the system under test, the task and environment, the granularity of judgment, and the cost of obtaining the score. This also explains why "adding one more metric" often fails to solve the problem: more metrics do not automatically bring evidence that can be located.

## Putting benchmarks back into questions: A quick-reference table

The most common thing that happens after reading the survey is that you remember a string of benchmark names and still do not know where to start when facing your own evaluation needs. This section organizes the material the other way round: first write down the question you want to answer, then find the corresponding benchmarks, and finally see what they leave uncovered. The benchmarks in the table all come from the survey's text, and the conditions are summarized from the original's descriptions. [§2–§5, PDF pp. 3–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16416v1#page=3)

| Question to answer | Can start with | Conditions it fixes | Evidence still needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can the model choose tools and fill in arguments? | BFCL, ComplexFuncBench, ToolSandbox | Tool set, whether arguments are stated, whether state changes | Success rate on complete tasks and call cost |
| Can a multi-step plan be carried through? | PlanBench, Natural Plan, MINT | Plan length, environmental feedback, available tools | End-to-end completion and recovery after errors |
| Can tasks be completed on real websites? | WebArena, WebCanvas, ST-WebAgentBench | Site snapshots or online environments, key nodes, policies | Policy compliance, repeated runs, changes to sites |
| Can problems in a real repository be fixed? | The SWE-bench family | Repository snapshot, problem description, validation tests | Strength of tests, answer leakage, multi-file changes |
| Which step of a scientific workflow can be done? | ScienceAgentBench, CORE-Bench, MLGym | Source of tasks, executable artifacts, domain | Scientific validity of conclusions, expert review |
| Can a conversational service get things done under policy? | τ-bench, ALMITA, IntellAgent | Database, interfaces, domain policy, simulated user | Process compliance, fluctuation from the simulated user |
| Distribution and transfer of general abilities | GAIA, AgentBench, OSWorld, TheAgentCompany | Task mixture, interfaces, environment types | Subscores, weighting, cost per task |
| Which step to blame for an error during development | Evaluation frameworks (Table 1) | Self-built data, scoring standards, trajectory records | Judge reliability, human in the loop, version updates |

The use of this table is to narrow the scope, not to give conclusions. After choosing a benchmark, go back to its original paper and check four things: where the tasks come from, whether the environment actually executes actions, whether judgment falls on outcomes or processes, and whether the two sides being compared ran under the same conditions. What the survey provides is precisely a coordinate system for classifying these four things; specific values and scales still have to be checked in the originals.

To continue reading, the order can be arranged like this: first use the [HELM guide](/read/helm/3#u02) to fill in the evaluation protocol of "scenario–adaptation–metric" and understand how a score is composed of several kinds of metric; then read [AgentBench](/read/agentbench/2#overall-score) to see how cross-environment scores are normalized and aggregated; then read [τ-bench](/read/tau-bench/2#passk) to see clearly the differences among the final state, process requirements and repeated runs; and finally use the [LLM evaluation survey guide](/read/llm-evaluation-survey/2#framework) to put the questions "what to evaluate, where and how" back into a more general evaluation framework. This is an editorially suggested reading order and does not imply that one paper provides experimental evidence for the next.
